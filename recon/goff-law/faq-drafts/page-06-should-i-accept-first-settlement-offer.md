# Should I Accept the First Settlement Offer From the Insurance Company?

By [Jim Goff](/attorneys/jim-goff/) | Updated 2026-05-16

Should I accept the first settlement offer? Almost never. The first offer from an insurance company is designed to be low — that's how the system works. Once you sign and accept, the case is permanently closed. This guide explains why first offers are anchored low, what "full and final" really means, and the four questions you must answer before signing anything.

## The short answer: almost never

In a Texas personal injury claim, the first settlement offer is rarely the fair number. It is an opening move in a negotiation — usually thrown out within days or weeks of the crash, before your medical picture is clear and before you've had time to think.

There are narrow exceptions. If you have **no injuries**, **no missed work**, and the offer fully covers your property damage and a small inconvenience payment, you may be fine taking it. But if you're hurt — even with a soft-tissue injury that "doesn't seem that bad" yet — that first number is almost certainly leaving real money on the table.

The reason is simple: insurance companies make money by paying claims for less than they're worth. The first offer is the test. If you take it, the file closes cheap and the adjuster gets credit. If you push back, they have authority to go higher — often much higher.

## Why the first offer is designed to be low

Insurance adjusters are trained negotiators. They use a tactic called **anchoring** — putting a low number out first so every counter-offer is measured against it. Behavioral research is clear: the first number in a negotiation sets the psychological frame, even when both sides know it's not the final figure.

A few specific reasons your first offer is low:

- **Your medical treatment isn't finished.** Most serious injuries — disc herniations, concussions, soft tissue injuries — show their true cost over weeks or months. An adjuster who settles fast captures you before that picture develops.
- **Future medical needs aren't on the table.** Physical therapy, injections, surgery, or long-term care can dwarf your current bills. They won't volunteer to pay for treatment you haven't had yet.
- **Lost earning capacity is ignored.** If your injury affects your ability to work in the future — even part-time, even temporarily — that's a damage category. The first offer doesn't include it.
- **Pain and suffering is undervalued.** Adjusters use internal software (Colossus is the best known) that produces conservative pain-and-suffering numbers. Juries often value the same injury at 2-5x the software's output.
- **You're under financial pressure.** They know medical bills are arriving. They know you may be missing work. That pressure is the leverage. The first offer is timed to use it.

## What "full and final" really means (and why it's permanent)

Every settlement agreement contains a **release** — usually titled "Full and Final Release of All Claims." Once you sign it, the case is over. Permanently. There is no going back, no reopening, no "I didn't know I'd need surgery."

Here's what that release legally extinguishes:

- All claims for **medical bills you've already incurred**
- All claims for **future medical treatment**, even if you don't know you'll need it
- All claims for **lost wages**, past and future
- All claims for **pain and suffering**, past and future
- All claims for **disability or impairment** discovered later
- Often, claims against **anyone connected to the at-fault party** — employers, vehicle owners, sometimes even unknown parties

If you develop a herniated disc three months after signing and need a $80,000 surgery, that is now your problem. The insurance company owes you nothing. Texas courts enforce these releases strictly. There are very narrow exceptions for fraud or mutual mistake, and they are extremely hard to prove.

This is why no responsible attorney lets a client settle before reaching **maximum medical improvement (MMI)** — the point where doctors can say with reasonable certainty what your future care will look like.

## The 4 questions to ask before accepting any offer

Before you sign anything, write down your answers to these four questions. If you can't answer all four with confidence, do not sign yet.

**1. Have I reached maximum medical improvement, or do my doctors expect more treatment?**

If you're still in treatment — or your doctors expect you to need more — the offer cannot account for those costs. You're signing away your right to be paid for treatment you haven't had yet.

**2. Does this offer cover ALL of my categories of damages?**

Make a list: past medical bills, future medical bills, past lost wages, future lost earning capacity, out-of-pocket costs (mileage, prescriptions, medical equipment), and pain and suffering. If the offer doesn't account for each category, you're leaving money behind.

**3. Have I subtracted what I'll actually owe out of the settlement?**

Health insurance subrogation, hospital liens, ERISA plans, Medicare/Medicaid — these all get paid out of your settlement. A $50,000 offer might net you $15,000 after liens and bills. Run the math BEFORE you accept, not after.

**4. Have I talked to a Texas personal injury attorney for a second opinion?**

A free consultation costs you nothing. A bad settlement costs you the difference for the rest of your life. If an attorney looks at your offer and says "that's actually fair, take it" — great, now you know. If they say "you're being underpaid by $30,000," now you really know.

## How counter-offers actually work in Texas PI cases

A settlement negotiation is not a single offer-and-acceptance. It's a back-and-forth that typically goes through 3–6 rounds before either side lands at a number both can live with.

The basic rhythm:

1. **Demand letter from your attorney** — lays out liability, damages, and a settlement number with supporting documentation (medical records, bills, wage loss proof, expert opinions).
2. **First insurance offer** — often 25–40% of demand. Adjusters call this "testing the demand."
3. **Counter from your attorney** — usually a meaningful but not dramatic drop from the original demand.
4. **Second offer** — typically 50–65% of demand if liability is clear.
5. **Continued negotiation** — may go 2–3 more rounds, often involving supervisor authority requests by the adjuster.
6. **Resolution or litigation** — if negotiation stalls, filing suit changes the adjuster's authority and often unlocks fair numbers.

In Texas, filing a lawsuit doesn't mean you're going to trial. Roughly 95% of filed PI cases still settle — but they settle for more after suit is filed because the insurance company now faces real exposure (jury risk, defense costs, time pressure).

## When a "lowball" can become a fair offer — and when it can't

Most low offers can be moved. Some can't. Here's how to tell the difference:

**Movable lowballs** usually share these traits:
- Liability is clear (the other driver was clearly at fault)
- Injuries are documented with consistent medical records
- Treatment is finished or near-finished
- There's a sympathetic story (clear victim, careful driver, real impact on life)
- The defendant has adequate insurance coverage

**Hard cases** — where even great lawyering struggles to move the number — include:
- Disputed liability (both drivers blaming each other)
- Gaps in medical treatment (you went weeks without seeing a doctor)
- Pre-existing conditions that complicate causation
- A low-impact crash with claimed serious injuries (insurance fights these hard)
- Minimum policy limits ($30,000 in Texas) with damages that exceed coverage

If your case has hard-case factors, that doesn't mean you can't recover — it means you need a strategy and an experienced lawyer to execute it. The first offer in a hard case isn't necessarily a lowball; it might be the realistic ceiling given the obstacles. A good attorney will tell you the difference honestly.
