# Who Pays Medical Bills After a Car Accident in Texas?

By [Jim Goff](/attorneys/jim-goff/) | Updated 2026-05-16

Here is the painful truth most Texas drivers never hear until they are sitting in an emergency room with a bill in hand: **in Texas, you pay your medical bills first and get reimbursed later.** The at-fault driver's insurance company does not cut a check the day after the crash. They wait until your treatment is finished and your case is settled, which can take months or years. In the meantime, the bills land on you. This guide explains who actually pays for your care while your claim is pending, and how a Texas personal injury lawyer can structure your treatment so you owe nothing out of pocket.

## Short answer: you pay first, then get reimbursed (here's why)

Texas is a fault-based insurance state. That means the driver who caused the crash is legally responsible for your medical expenses, but their insurance company has no obligation to pay anything until liability is established and damages are documented. In practice, they will not release a single dollar until you sign a full release of your claim. So even though your case is worth what your treatment costs, the cash flow does not work in your favor.

While the case is pending, your medical bills get routed through whatever coverage you actually have in place at the time of the crash. That usually means some combination of Personal Injury Protection (PIP), MedPay, your own health insurance, and in serious cases, a lawyer-arranged Letter of Protection. Each option has tradeoffs. Understanding how they stack is the difference between getting full treatment and going broke trying to recover.

## PIP coverage: Texas's $2,500 default + opt-out

Personal Injury Protection is the closest thing Texas has to no-fault coverage. Under [Texas Insurance Code § 1952.152](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/IN/htm/IN.1952.htm), every auto insurance policy sold in Texas must include at least $2,500 in PIP coverage **unless the policyholder rejects it in writing**. Most drivers never read the rejection form, which is good news — it means you probably have at least $2,500 sitting there to pay your initial medical bills and a portion of lost wages.

PIP is no-fault, meaning it pays regardless of who caused the crash. It also covers passengers in your vehicle. The money typically arrives within 30 days of submitting medical bills to your own insurer. You can buy higher PIP limits — $5,000, $10,000, even $25,000 — and if you live in DFW and drive daily, this is one of the smartest insurance upgrades you can make. The premium is usually under $100 a year for a meaningful increase.

The catch: if you signed a written PIP rejection (often buried in your application), you have $0 in PIP. Pull your declarations page and look. If you cannot find "Personal Injury Protection" listed with a dollar amount, you waived it.

## MedPay vs. PIP: what's different

MedPay (medical payments coverage) is similar to PIP but narrower. Both are no-fault and pay your medical bills regardless of who caused the crash. The key differences:

- **PIP covers medical bills AND lost wages AND replacement services** (someone to mow the lawn while you recover). MedPay only covers medical bills.
- **MedPay typically has higher limits available** — $1,000 to $25,000 or more.
- **MedPay does not require written rejection** — you either bought it or you didn't.

In DFW, many drivers carry both. If you have $5,000 in PIP and $5,000 in MedPay, you have $10,000 in immediate medical coverage that pays no matter who was at fault, no matter who was driving your car, and no matter what the at-fault driver's insurance ends up doing.

## Health insurance: subrogation rights and what they take back

Your health insurance will pay for your accident-related medical care just like any other claim. Use it. Do not let an emergency room or doctor's office tell you they cannot bill your health plan because it was a car accident — that is sometimes a billing-department reflex, not the law.

Here is the catch most people miss: most health insurance plans have **subrogation rights**. That means when your case settles, the health insurer can demand reimbursement for what they paid. If Blue Cross paid $40,000 toward your hospital bill and you settle for $150,000, Blue Cross can claim part of that settlement to recoup their $40,000.

A few important nuances:
- **Self-funded ERISA plans** (most large-employer plans) have the strongest subrogation rights and often get paid back dollar-for-dollar.
- **Fully-insured Texas plans** are subject to Texas's "made whole" doctrine, which can reduce or eliminate the lien if you did not recover full damages.
- **Medicare and Medicaid** have federal lien rights that must be resolved before a case can close.

A lawyer's job here is to negotiate these liens down. It is routine in Texas for skilled negotiators to reduce health insurance reimbursement claims by 30% to 50%, which goes directly to your net recovery.

## The at-fault driver's insurance: when (and how) they pay

The at-fault driver's bodily injury liability coverage is what most people think of as "the other driver's insurance paying my medical bills." It does pay — but not until the end of the case. The at-fault carrier will demand:

1. All medical records and itemized bills
2. Proof of lost wages
3. Evidence the crash caused the injuries (medical causation opinion)
4. A signed release of all claims in exchange for payment

This process takes 6 to 18 months for a typical case, longer for serious injuries. The at-fault carrier writes one check, you sign one release, and the case is closed. You cannot go back later if your injuries get worse. That is why early settlement offers are dangerous — your bills may keep coming in for years.

Texas minimum bodily injury liability is only **$30,000 per person / $60,000 per accident**. Many DFW drivers carry exactly that minimum. If your hospital bill alone is $80,000, the at-fault driver's policy will not cover it — which brings us to the next coverage.

## What happens if the other driver is uninsured (UM/UIM coverage)

Uninsured Motorist (UM) and Underinsured Motorist (UIM) coverage is the single most important coverage on your own policy for a serious crash. It pays when:

- The other driver has no insurance (about 20% of Texas drivers)
- The other driver has insurance but not enough (very common with $30K minimum policies)
- A hit-and-run driver cannot be identified

UM/UIM is your own insurance company paying for the at-fault driver's failure to carry enough coverage. Like PIP, Texas requires insurers to offer it and requires written rejection to waive it. Check your declarations page — if you do not see UM/UIM with a dollar amount, you likely waived it.

In DFW, where minimum-limits drivers are everywhere, having $100,000 or more in UM/UIM is the cheapest catastrophic-injury insurance you can buy. Premium impact is typically $10 to $30 per month for limits that could save your financial life.

## Letters of protection: how lawyers get treatment without upfront cost

If you have no PIP, no health insurance, and the at-fault driver's coverage is in dispute, you still need medical treatment — and you need it documented for your case. This is where a **Letter of Protection (LOP)** comes in.

An LOP is a written promise from your attorney to the medical provider that says: "Treat my client now. Send the bill to me. I will pay you out of the settlement proceeds when the case resolves." It allows you to see specialists, get MRIs, undergo surgery, and complete physical therapy with $0 out of pocket.

Not every provider accepts LOPs — but in DFW, a network of orthopedists, neurologists, pain-management physicians, surgical centers, and imaging facilities work routinely with personal injury attorneys on LOP terms. Knowing which providers accept LOPs and which ones bill responsibly is part of what a Texas PI lawyer brings to the table on day one.

One word of caution: LOP treatment bills sometimes come in higher than insurance-negotiated rates. A good lawyer negotiates those bills down at the end of the case, the same way they negotiate health-insurance subrogation claims. The net result for you should be better — full treatment, no upfront cost, fair lien resolution.
