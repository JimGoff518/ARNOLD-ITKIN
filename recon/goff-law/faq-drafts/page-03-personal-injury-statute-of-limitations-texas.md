# How Long Do I Have To File a Personal Injury Claim in Texas? (Statute of Limitations Guide)

By [Jim Goff](/attorneys/jim-goff/) | Updated 2026-05-16

If you are asking how long do I have to file a personal injury claim in Texas, the short answer is **2 years from the date of the injury** under Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.003. But that simple rule has real exceptions — and at least one trap (government claims) that can shrink your window to as little as 6 months. Miss the deadline and your case is over, no matter how strong the facts. Here is the full picture.

## The 2-year rule: Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.003

Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.003(a) sets the standard deadline: "a person must bring suit for ... personal injury ... not later than two years after the day the cause of action accrues." That covers almost everything we handle — car wrecks, truck collisions, rideshare crashes, product injuries, dangerous-condition injuries on commercial property, and motorcycle wrecks.

"Bring suit" means actually file the lawsuit in the right court — not just send a demand letter, not just hire a lawyer, not just open a claim with the insurance company. The clerk's office has to stamp the petition. If day 730 passes and no lawsuit has been filed, your right to sue is gone forever.

There are no extensions for being busy, being in treatment, or trying to negotiate with the insurance company. Insurance adjusters know this. There is a documented playbook of insurance companies stringing claimants along with friendly emails for 22 months, then going dark for the last 2 months until the deadline runs out. Do not let that happen.

## When the 2-year clock starts (and when it pauses)

The 2-year clock starts on the date your cause of action "accrues." For a car wreck, that is the date of the crash. Simple.

For some claims, accrual is less obvious. The Texas Supreme Court has held that accrual happens when the wrongful act causes the legal injury — not when the injury becomes severe or you connect the dots. That is why the discovery rule (below) matters.

A few situations pause ("toll") the clock under Texas law:

- **Minor under 18.** § 16.001 tolls the clock until the minor turns 18 (then they have 2 years from their 18th birthday).
- **Mental incapacity at the time of injury.** Tolled while the incapacity continues.
- **Defendant leaves Texas.** Some periods of absence from Texas can toll the clock under § 16.063.

Outside those narrow situations, assume the clock runs continuously. Holidays, weekends, and "I was waiting for the insurance company to respond" do not stop it.

## Exceptions: minors, mental incapacity, discovery rule

**Minors.** A child injured in a car wreck at age 10 has until age 20 to file (2 years after turning 18). But parents can — and usually should — file on behalf of the child sooner, both to preserve evidence and to access medical treatment funding. Wrongful-death claims involving a minor's death have different rules.

**Mental incapacity.** If the injured person was legally incapacitated at the time of the injury (severe brain injury, coma, pre-existing condition rendering them incompetent), the clock is tolled until the incapacity is removed. Voluntary intoxication does not count.

**The discovery rule.** In certain narrow Texas cases — primarily latent disease or hidden defect cases — the clock starts when the plaintiff knew or reasonably should have known of the injury. The Texas Supreme Court has limited this rule sharply; it is the exception, not the norm. Asbestos, certain toxic exposures, and some fraud-based claims qualify. A car wreck does not.

This matters for federal MDL mass torts (defective hernia mesh, certain hair-relaxer cancer claims, defective medical devices). If you developed symptoms years after exposure, you may still have time — but call a lawyer before assuming you do.

## Wrongful death: same 2 years, different start date

Texas wrongful death claims under § 16.003(b) also have a 2-year deadline, but the clock starts on the **date of death**, not the date of the underlying injury.

Example: A truck wreck on January 1, 2024 puts a family member into a coma. They pass away on August 15, 2024. The personal injury claim deadline is January 1, 2026 (2 years from the crash). The wrongful death claim deadline is August 15, 2026 (2 years from death). Both clocks can run at the same time, on the same facts.

Wrongful death actions can only be brought by the surviving spouse, children, or parents. Siblings, grandparents, and unmarried partners do not have standing in Texas. A survival action under § 71.021 lets the estate also pursue the decedent's pre-death pain and suffering — also subject to a 2-year deadline from death.

## Claims against government entities: 6-month notice rule (much shorter)

This is the trap. If your injury involves a city, county, the State of Texas, or any government employee acting in their official capacity (think city dump truck, DART bus, county sheriff's vehicle, government road defect), you are governed by the Texas Tort Claims Act — and you must give **written notice within 6 months** under Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 101.101. Some cities have even shorter charter deadlines.

Examples of short city deadlines you need to verify:
- **Dallas:** 90 days written notice required under city charter.
- **Fort Worth:** Written notice within 6 months.
- **Plano, Frisco, Arlington, McKinney:** Charter or ordinance deadlines may apply — verify the specific deadline immediately.

If you miss the notice deadline, your claim is barred even if you are still well within the 2-year statute. The notice has to include time, place, description of the incident, and damages. There is no informal fix and "I didn't know" is not an excuse.

If a government vehicle, bus, road condition, or employee may have been involved in your injury, talk to a lawyer in the first 30 days. Period.

## Why "I'll wait and see" almost always backfires

"I'll wait until I'm done with treatment" is the most common reason people miss their deadline. Two years sounds like plenty of time until you actually try to investigate, gather records, value a case, send a demand letter, negotiate, fail to settle, draft a petition, and file in the right court — all in the right order.

Three things to remember:

1. **Evidence disappears fast.** Surveillance footage gets erased in 7 to 30 days at most businesses. Truck driver logs can be "lost" after 6 months. Witness memories fade.
2. **Lawyers need lead time.** Most reputable Texas PI lawyers will not take a case with under 60 days left on the statute, because we cannot properly investigate, demand, and file in that window. Wait too long and no one will take it.
3. **Filing is not negotiating.** Filing a lawsuit does not mean you go to trial. It just preserves your right. Most cases still settle after filing — but filing on time is what keeps the settlement leverage alive.

If you are unsure how much time you have, call a lawyer this week. The consultation is free and the calendar is unforgiving.

## When to call a Texas personal injury lawyer

If you were injured in Texas and any of the following is true, do not wait:
- The injury involves a government vehicle, road, or employee (6-month clock).
- The injury involves a minor (file early to preserve evidence).
- You are more than 12 months past the date of injury.
- You believe the discovery rule might apply (mass tort, latent injury).

A free consultation costs nothing. Missing the deadline costs everything.
