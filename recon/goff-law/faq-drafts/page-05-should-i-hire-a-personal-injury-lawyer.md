# Should I Hire a Personal Injury Lawyer? (Honest Guide)

By [Jim Goff](/attorneys/jim-goff/) | Updated 2026-05-16

Should I hire a personal injury lawyer? The honest answer is: not always. Some claims are small enough that you can handle them yourself and keep 100% of the check. But in most cases involving real injuries, the data shows represented claimants recover significantly more — even after attorney fees. This guide tells you both sides so you can make the call that's right for your situation.

## When you probably don't need a lawyer (and we'll tell you so)

We turn cases away every week. If your situation looks like one of these, you can usually handle it on your own:

- **A true minor fender-bender with no injury.** You bumped, you stepped out, you felt fine then and you feel fine a week later. Property damage only. The other driver's insurer pays for your bumper and you move on.
- **Your own car is damaged but no one is hurt.** Property damage claims are paperwork, not litigation. Get three repair estimates, send them to the adjuster, and negotiate the gap yourself.
- **You were already paid fairly and the offer covers everything.** Rare, but it happens. If the offer covers all medical bills (past and future), lost wages, and pays something reasonable for the pain you went through, it may be fine.
- **The total claim value is under $2,000–$3,000.** A 33% contingency fee on a $1,500 claim leaves you with $1,000. You'd do better in small claims court or a direct negotiation.

If your case looks like one of these, call us anyway for a free 15-minute consult and we'll tell you so. That's a real promise.

## The 5 situations where you absolutely need one

On the other side, these five situations almost always need a lawyer — and waiting will cost you money:

1. **Any injury that required an ER visit, imaging (MRI/CT), or follow-up treatment.** Once medical bills start, the math changes fast. Hospital liens, health insurance subrogation, and future treatment estimates all need legal handling.
2. **Lost time from work — even a few days.** Lost wages and lost earning capacity are categories adjusters consistently underpay without an attorney involved.
3. **The other driver was uninsured, underinsured, or fled the scene.** Now you're fighting your OWN insurance company for UM/UIM coverage. They are not on your side once a claim opens.
4. **Commercial vehicle, truck, or rideshare crashes.** Multiple insurance policies, federal regulations, and corporate defense lawyers are involved from day one. You are outgunned without counsel.
5. **The insurance company is denying liability, blaming you, or delaying.** This is a signal that they intend to lowball or stonewall. A lawyer changes that calculus immediately.

## The data: represented claimants recover 3x or more on average

This isn't a sales pitch. It's a number that comes from the **Insurance Research Council (IRC) Auto Injury Claims Studies** — research published by the insurance industry itself. Their multi-year studies have consistently found that claimants represented by an attorney recover roughly **3x or more** what unrepresented claimants recover for similar injuries.

Even after a standard 33⅓% contingency fee, represented claimants end up with substantially more money in their pocket. The math works because lawyers know which damages to demand (future medical, diminished earning capacity, household services, pain and suffering), how to document them, and when to file suit to force a fair number.

The IRC isn't a plaintiff's group. They are funded by the insurance industry. When their own research says representation increases recovery, that's worth knowing.

## What insurance adjusters won't tell you

Insurance adjusters are not bad people. They are people doing a job — and their job is to close claims for as little money as possible. Here's what they don't volunteer:

- **Your first offer is a starting point, not a final number.** They expect you to counter. If you accept the first offer, they note it in your file and tighten the screws on the next person.
- **Medical bills are negotiable.** They may quote you "billed charges" instead of what insurance actually paid, which is often 30–60% lower. That number matters for how damages are calculated.
- **Future medical costs are part of your claim.** If you'll need physical therapy, injections, or surgery down the road, you can claim those costs now — but only if a doctor documents the need and you know to demand it.
- **A recorded statement can sink your case.** Adjusters often ask for one in the first week. There's almost never a legal requirement to give one to the other driver's insurer. Many cases are damaged by a single sentence said too early.

## What to look for in a Texas PI lawyer (beyond TV ads)

Big billboards and bus benches do not equal results. When you're evaluating a Texas personal injury attorney, ask about:

- **Texas trial experience.** Settlement-mill firms move volume; trial-ready firms move numbers. Ask: "Have you tried a case in front of a Texas jury in the last 2 years?"
- **Who will actually handle your case?** At many firms, you sign with a name partner and never see them again. Ask who your day-to-day point of contact will be.
- **Caseload per attorney.** A lawyer juggling 300 cases cannot give yours the attention it needs. Ask the number.
- **Practice focus.** A general practitioner who occasionally does PI is not the same as a firm built around it. Specialization matters.
- **Communication standard.** Ask how often you'll get updates and how quickly calls/texts are returned. Get a real answer, not a marketing line.

## Free consultations: what to ask in the first call

Almost every reputable Texas PI firm offers a free consultation. Make it count. Bring:

- The police/crash report (CR-3) if you have it
- All medical records and bills so far
- Photos from the scene
- Any communication from the other driver's insurance

Then ask:

1. **"Based on what I've told you, is my case worth pursuing?"** A trustworthy attorney will say no when no is the right answer.
2. **"What is your fee structure, and what expenses come out of my recovery?"** Get it in plain numbers, not percentages without context.
3. **"What is the realistic range of outcomes for a case like mine?"** A lawyer who promises a specific dollar figure on day one is selling, not advising.
4. **"How long do you expect this to take?"** Most Texas PI cases resolve in 6–18 months. Outliers go longer. You deserve a real timeline.
5. **"What can I do — or avoid doing — to help my case right now?"** Their answer tells you how engaged they'll be.

If you walk out of the consult feeling rushed, pressured, or unclear about the next step, that's information. Trust it.
