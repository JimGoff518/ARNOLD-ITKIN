# What To Do After a Car Accident in Texas: A Step-by-Step Guide

By [Jim Goff](/attorneys/jim-goff/) | Updated 2026-05-16

If you just got rear-ended on I-35 or T-boned at a Plano intersection, you are probably running on adrenaline and not thinking clearly. That is normal. This guide walks you through exactly what to do after a car accident in Texas — in the order you need to do it — so you protect your health first and your legal claim second. Everything below is what I would tell a family member to do if they called me from the scene.

## Step 1: Make sure everyone is safe and call 911

Before anything else, check yourself and your passengers for injuries. If anyone is hurt or unconscious, do not move them unless the car is on fire or in a live traffic lane. Call 911 immediately. In Texas, you are required by law to call 911 if there is any injury, any death, or visible vehicle damage that looks meaningful.

If the cars are drivable and nobody is hurt, move them to the shoulder or a parking lot. Texas Transportation Code § 550.022 actually requires you to move vehicles out of a moving traffic lane after a non-injury crash if it is safe to do so. Turn on your hazards. Set out flares or triangles if you have them.

Stay at the scene. Leaving the scene of a Texas crash with injuries is a felony — even if you think it was not your fault. Wait for police. When they arrive, give them facts only. Do not guess at speeds, do not estimate distances, and do not apologize. Just tell them what you saw.

## Step 2: What to say (and NOT say) to the other driver

Texas law requires you to exchange information with the other driver. That means name, address, driver's license number, license plate, and insurance information. That is it. You are not required to discuss fault, and you should not.

Say this: "Are you okay? Let's exchange information and wait for the police." That is the entire script.

Do NOT say any of the following, even if you feel them:
- "I'm sorry."
- "I didn't see you."
- "I think I might have been speeding."
- "I'm fine, no injuries."

Here is why that last one matters. Adrenaline masks pain for hours, sometimes days. Soft tissue injuries, concussions, and disc injuries usually show up the next morning. If you tell the other driver or the responding officer "I'm fine," that statement ends up in the crash report and the insurance company will use it against you for the rest of your case. Stick to facts. If you do not know, say you do not know.

## Step 3: Texas crash-reporting rules — when CR-3 is required

The CR-3 is the official Texas Peace Officer's Crash Report. When a police officer responds to your wreck, the officer fills out the CR-3 within 10 days and files it with the Texas Department of Transportation. You can usually buy a copy from TxDOT's online portal for about $6 to $8 about 10 business days after the crash.

Get a copy. The CR-3 is the single most important document in the first 30 days of your case. It contains the officer's opinion on contributing factors, a diagram of the crash, witness names, and citation information. Insurance adjusters read it before they decide anything.

What if no officer shows up? It happens, especially on minor crashes during busy hours. In that case, Texas used to require drivers to self-report on a form called the CR-2 (the "blue form"). Texas eliminated the mandatory CR-2 in 2017, but you should still document the crash yourself in writing the same day. Even better: drive to the nearest police station and ask them to take a delayed report. Many DFW agencies will.

Texas has a 10-day clock on the officer's report. If you do not have a CR-3 within two weeks, follow up — sometimes reports get misfiled.

## Step 4: Photograph everything — the 8-shot checklist

Your phone is the best evidence-gathering tool you own. Before the tow truck arrives, take these 8 shots:

1. **Wide shot of the entire scene** from where you were standing — shows the road, both cars, and traffic context.
2. **All four corners of your vehicle**, even the undamaged sides (insurance loves to claim pre-existing damage).
3. **Close-up of every dent, scrape, and broken part** on your car and the other car.
4. **The other driver's license plate** and a wide shot of their vehicle.
5. **Skid marks, debris, fluid leaks, and final resting positions** of both cars.
6. **Traffic signals, stop signs, or lane markings** that are relevant — especially if a signal was malfunctioning.
7. **Your own injuries** — bruises, cuts, airbag burns, seatbelt marks. Re-shoot these for 7 days because bruising peaks at 48 to 72 hours.
8. **The other driver's insurance card and driver's license** (ask politely; this is standard).

Bonus: shoot a slow 360-degree video while narrating what you see. Date and time get embedded in the file metadata.

Also write down the names and phone numbers of any witnesses on the spot. Witnesses disappear within 48 hours and the police report does not always capture them.

## Step 5: Get medical attention within 72 hours (why this matters for your claim)

If you have any pain, stiffness, headache, dizziness, numbness, or just feel "off" after a wreck — go to an ER, urgent care, or your primary doctor within 72 hours. Do not wait a week to "see if it gets better." There are two reasons.

First, medical. Concussions, internal bleeding, and disc injuries can be serious even when symptoms feel mild at first. Get checked.

Second, legal. Insurance adjusters look at the gap between the crash date and your first doctor's visit. If you waited 10 days to see anyone, the adjuster will argue that whatever you are now claiming was caused by something else — not the wreck. A 72-hour visit creates a clean medical record tying your injury to the crash. That single visit can be worth tens of thousands of dollars in claim value down the road.

Tell the doctor every symptom, even the small ones. If you do not mention a headache at visit one, then bring it up four weeks later, the insurance company argues you made it up.

## Step 6: Notify your insurance — what to say in the first 48 hours

Texas auto policies require "prompt notice" of any accident. That usually means within 24 to 48 hours. Call your own insurance and report the crash. Stick to the same script as the scene: facts only.

Tell your insurance:
- When and where the crash happened.
- The other driver's name, plate, and insurance.
- That a police report was filed (or not).
- That you sought medical attention (or are about to).

Do NOT do these things:
- Do not give a recorded statement to the OTHER driver's insurance company. Ever. They will call you within 24 hours and they will be very friendly. You are not legally required to talk to them, and anything you say will be used to reduce your claim.
- Do not accept any settlement offer in the first 30 days, no matter how reasonable it sounds. The full extent of your injuries is not known yet.
- Do not sign a medical authorization that gives them access to your entire medical history. They only need records related to this crash.

If the other driver's insurance calls, tell them: "I am not giving a statement. Please direct all communication to my attorney." Then hang up and call one.

## Step 7: When to call a Texas personal injury lawyer (and why waiting hurts your case)

You should call a personal injury lawyer in any of these situations:
- You went to the ER, urgent care, or a doctor.
- You missed work, even one day.
- The other driver was cited or appears to have been drunk, distracted, or speeding.
- The crash involved a commercial truck, rideshare (Uber/Lyft), or government vehicle.
- The other driver is uninsured or underinsured.
- The insurance company is pressuring you to settle quickly.

Every day you wait costs you evidence. Skid marks fade. Surveillance footage gets overwritten after 7 to 30 days. Witnesses forget. Vehicles get repaired or scrapped, destroying crash data. The Event Data Recorder ("black box") in modern cars holds about 20 seconds of crash data — and it can be lost if the car gets totaled and crushed.

A lawyer can send preservation letters, pull 911 audio, subpoena nearby business cameras, and lock down the truck driver's logs before the trucking company "loses" them. None of that is possible 60 days later.

Calling a lawyer is free. Hiring one is free too — Texas personal injury attorneys work on contingency, which means $0 upfront and they only get paid if you win. There is no downside to a free consultation.
