# How Much Can You Sue For Pain and Suffering in Texas?

By [Jim Goff](/attorneys/jim-goff/) | Updated 2026-05-16

If you are asking **how much you can sue for pain and suffering in Texas**, the honest answer is: it depends on the injury, the evidence, and whether your case falls under one of the narrow categories where Texas caps non-economic damages. The good news is that the vast majority of personal injury cases — including car accidents, truck crashes, and product injuries — have **no cap on pain and suffering**. Awards in Texas can range from low five figures for a soft-tissue case to seven figures or more for catastrophic injuries. This guide explains how that math actually works, what counts as pain and suffering, and what you can do to document your case for the highest possible award.

## Pain and suffering damages: what counts in Texas

"Pain and suffering" is a legal shorthand for non-economic damages — the harms that do not show up on a hospital bill. In Texas, juries are allowed to compensate you for:

- **Physical pain** — both past and future
- **Mental anguish** — anxiety, depression, PTSD, fear, humiliation
- **Physical impairment** — loss of the ability to do things you used to do (run, lift, play with your kids)
- **Disfigurement** — scars, amputations, visible changes to your body
- **Loss of consortium** — impact on the spousal relationship (in serious-injury cases)

These are real damages. They are recoverable in Texas, and juries award them every day. The challenge is that, unlike a medical bill or a paycheck, there is no receipt for pain. So lawyers and insurance adjusters use a few standard methods to translate suffering into a dollar amount.

## The multiplier method (1.5x – 5x medical bills)

The most common starting framework — used by insurance adjusters, plaintiffs' lawyers, and mediators across Texas — is the **multiplier method**. You take the total medical bills (economic damages) and multiply by a number that reflects the severity and permanence of the injury.

- **1.5x to 2x** — minor soft-tissue injuries, full recovery, no lasting effects
- **2x to 3x** — moderate injuries, several months of treatment, some lasting symptoms
- **3x to 4x** — serious injuries, surgery, ongoing pain or limitation
- **4x to 5x or higher** — catastrophic injuries, permanent disability, disfigurement, lifetime impact

So if your medical bills are $40,000 and you had surgery with a year of recovery and lingering nerve damage, a defense adjuster might value your pain and suffering at $80,000 to $120,000 (2x–3x). A skilled plaintiff's lawyer presenting the same case to a jury might argue for $160,000 to $200,000 (4x–5x). The gap between those numbers is where good lawyering lives.

The multiplier is not law. It is a negotiating convention. Juries do not have to use it, and in catastrophic cases they routinely award amounts that no multiplier would have predicted.

## The per-diem method (and when juries use it)

The per-diem ("per day") method assigns a dollar value to each day you suffered. A common framework: take your daily wages, treat that as the value of a "lost day" of normal life, and multiply by the number of days from injury to maximum medical recovery.

If you earn $250 a day and you suffered for 600 days before reaching maximum recovery, the per-diem calculation produces $150,000 in pain and suffering. Plaintiffs' lawyers often use per-diem in closing arguments because it forces the jury to think about what each day of pain is actually worth. It is especially effective for chronic-pain and long-recovery cases.

In settlement negotiations, the per-diem and multiplier methods are usually used as cross-checks — if both methods produce numbers in the same range, you have a defensible value. If they diverge widely (common with permanent injuries), the higher number is usually closer to fair.

## Caps that apply: medical malpractice ($250K) — and why most cases have NO cap

Texas caps non-economic damages in **two narrow categories**:

1. **Medical malpractice** — non-economic damages capped at **$250,000** against a single provider, with overall caps for hospitals. Set by 2003 tort reform.
2. **Claims against governmental entities** — capped under the Texas Tort Claims Act ($250,000 per person / $500,000 per occurrence for most municipal claims).

**That's it.** Every other category of personal injury case — car accidents, truck crashes, motorcycle wrecks, commercial vehicle collisions, defective products, federal mass torts — has **no cap on pain and suffering** under Texas law. A jury can award what the evidence supports.

This matters for how you choose a lawyer. Goff Law DFW does not handle medical malpractice. We focus on motor vehicle injury cases and federal mass tort claims, both of which are uncapped. If a firm tells you Texas "caps pain and suffering at $250,000" for your car accident case, they are confusing medical malpractice law with general personal injury law. They are different statutes.

[Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code Chapter 41](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CP/htm/CP.41.htm) governs damages generally. The chapter restricts **exemplary (punitive) damages** to roughly 2x economic damages plus an amount equal to non-economic damages, capped at $750,000 — but it does not cap compensatory pain and suffering in ordinary negligence cases.

## How insurance adjusters value pain and suffering differently than juries

Insurance adjusters use proprietary software (Colossus, Claims IQ, and similar tools) to assign pain and suffering values based on diagnosis codes, treatment duration, and injury severity. These programs are designed to produce numbers that are **defensible at the low end of reasonable**. They do not capture jury reality.

Juries in Texas — especially in urban counties like Dallas and Tarrant — routinely award non-economic damages well above what adjusters first offer. Three drivers of this gap:

1. **Adjusters discount mental anguish.** Software does not handle PTSD, depression, or fear of driving well. Juries do.
2. **Adjusters discount future suffering.** Software focuses on what has happened, not what will happen for the rest of your life.
3. **Adjusters assume you will not file suit.** The first offer is calibrated to your most cost-effective acceptance, not to the case's verdict value.

The practical takeaway: the first offer on a serious injury case is almost never the offer you should accept. Cases that go to mediation after suit is filed routinely settle for two to five times the first pre-suit offer — because suddenly the carrier has to weigh real jury risk, not just statistical models.

## Documentation that increases pain-and-suffering awards

The difference between a four-figure pain-and-suffering settlement and a six-figure one is usually documentation. Concrete steps that help:

- **Tell every doctor about every symptom.** If your back hurts, your sleep is disrupted, and you can no longer carry your toddler, all three need to be in the medical record. If it is not in the chart, it did not happen.
- **Keep a daily pain journal.** A short daily note — pain level 1 to 10, what you could not do today, how you felt emotionally — becomes powerful evidence months later when memory fades.
- **See the right specialists.** Pain management doctors, physical therapists, neurologists, and orthopedists generate the kind of objective documentation juries find credible.
- **Get mental health treatment if you need it.** Anxiety and depression after a serious crash are common and compensable. A few sessions with a licensed counselor create a record of mental anguish.
- **Photograph your injuries throughout recovery.** Bruising fades, scars heal, casts come off. Photos taken weekly during recovery preserve what jurors otherwise never see.
- **Bring family members to depositions and trial.** Spouse and family testimony about how your daily life has changed is some of the most powerful pain-and-suffering evidence in a Texas courtroom.

## Range of awards by injury type

Without naming specific case results, the general ranges Texas juries and settlements produce for non-economic damages alone (separate from medical bills and lost wages):

- **Soft-tissue injuries with full recovery** — low five figures
- **Surgical orthopedic injuries with full recovery** — high five to low six figures
- **Permanent orthopedic injury with ongoing pain** — mid six figures
- **Traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury, severe burns** — high six figures to seven figures
- **Catastrophic / quadriplegic / wrongful death cases** — seven figures and higher

These are general ranges based on injury type and severity. Every case is different, and no lawyer can promise a specific outcome. Anyone who quotes you a dollar figure in a free consultation before reviewing your medical records is guessing.
