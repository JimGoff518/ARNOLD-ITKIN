# What To Do If You're Injured at Work in Texas (When It's NOT Workers' Comp)

By [Jim Goff](/attorneys/jim-goff/) | Updated 2026-05-16

> **Important disclaimer:** Goff Law DFW does **not** handle workers' compensation claims. If your injury is comp-only — meaning your employer carries Texas workers' comp insurance and no third party caused your injury — we'll refer you to a workers' comp specialist at no charge. This page covers what to do if you're injured at work in Texas and your case is **not** a standard workers' comp claim.

Most people assume "hurt at work" means "workers' comp." In Texas, that's often wrong. Texas is the only state where workers' comp is **optional** for private employers. On top of that, many serious work injuries are caused by someone other than your employer — a third party who can be sued under regular Texas negligence law. This guide explains when you have a claim outside the workers' comp system, and the deadlines you cannot afford to miss.

## When you can sue beyond workers' comp in Texas

There are four main situations where you have a personal injury claim in Texas that is separate from — or instead of — workers' comp:

1. **Your employer is a "non-subscriber"** — they don't carry workers' comp insurance. You can sue them directly under Texas negligence law.
2. **A third party caused your injury** — someone other than your employer or a co-worker. Common examples: another driver hit you while you were driving for work, a defective machine made by an outside manufacturer hurt you, a subcontractor on the same job site dropped equipment.
3. **You're a 1099 contractor**, not an employee. Workers' comp often doesn't apply at all. You may have other negligence remedies.
4. **Gross negligence resulting in death** — even when an employer has workers' comp, surviving family members can bring an exemplary damages claim for gross negligence under Texas Labor Code § 408.001(b).

These cases can recover money the workers' comp system simply does not pay — full pain and suffering, full lost earning capacity, full mental anguish, and (in death cases) exemplary damages. The numbers are often several times higher than what a comp claim would have paid for the same injury.

## The non-subscriber employer loophole (Texas is unique)

Texas is the only state in the country where private employers can legally **opt out** of the workers' compensation system. Employers who opt out are called **non-subscribers**. According to the Texas Department of Insurance, roughly 28% of Texas employers — and a much higher share of small businesses — choose to be non-subscribers.

Here's why this matters for an injured worker: under **Texas Labor Code § 406.033**, when a non-subscriber employer is sued for a workplace injury, the employer **loses three of its most powerful defenses**:

- **Contributory negligence is unavailable.** The employer cannot blame your own carelessness for the injury.
- **Assumption of risk is unavailable.** The employer cannot argue that you knew the job was dangerous and accepted that risk.
- **The fellow-servant doctrine is unavailable.** The employer cannot blame a co-worker for the injury.

The only thing the employer must prove is that you were not 100% at fault and that the employer's negligence caused — in any part — your injury. This is a much lower bar than a typical negligence case.

If you've been hurt and your employer doesn't have workers' comp, you may have a powerful claim. The first step is to find out whether your employer is a subscriber. You can check this directly with the **Texas Department of Insurance, Division of Workers' Compensation** at tdi.texas.gov — they maintain a free public database.

## Third-party negligence claims: when someone else caused your work injury

Even if your employer DOES have workers' comp, you may still have a separate lawsuit against a **third party** whose negligence caused the injury. Workers' comp doesn't block these claims. In fact, many of the largest work-injury recoveries in Texas come from third-party suits, not comp claims.

Common third-party scenarios:

- **You were driving for work and another driver hit you.** Delivery driver, sales rep, service tech, on the way to a job site — if a non-employer hit your vehicle, you have a regular auto negligence claim against that driver (and often their commercial insurer) on top of any comp benefits.
- **Defective equipment or product.** A press, saw, ladder, scaffolding, vehicle, or industrial tool that failed because it was defectively designed or manufactured. You can sue the manufacturer, distributor, or maintenance contractor under Texas products liability law.
- **Another contractor on a multi-employer worksite.** Construction, oilfield, and industrial sites usually have multiple contractors. If a different company's worker dropped a tool, ran a forklift into you, or created the hazard that hurt you, that company is fair game.
- **Negligent property owner.** If a property owner (not your employer) failed to maintain the premises and you were hurt there for work, premises liability law may apply.
- **Commercial trucks.** If you were hit by an 18-wheeler during work hours, federal trucking regulations and corporate insurance policies open up significantly larger recoveries.

The big advantage of a third-party claim: full damages. No caps on lost wages. No caps on pain and suffering for the at-work portion. Real money for real injuries.

## 1099 contractor injuries — workers' comp may not apply at all

If you work as a **1099 independent contractor**, you are usually not covered by your hiring company's workers' comp policy — even if you're hurt while doing their work. That's the legal reality, but it cuts both ways.

The downside: no automatic comp benefits if you're hurt.

The upside: you are **free to sue** the hiring company under regular Texas negligence law if their negligence caused your injury. None of the workers' comp exclusivity rules apply to you.

A few things to know:

- **Misclassification is common.** Many workers labeled "1099" by their employer are legally **employees** under Texas and federal tests (control, equipment, training, integration). If you've been misclassified, you may have additional claims and may actually be covered by comp.
- **Hold-harmless clauses are not always enforceable.** Many 1099 contracts try to make you waive injury claims. Texas law limits how far those waivers can go, especially for gross negligence.
- **You may still have a third-party claim** even if you don't have one against the hiring company — for example, if defective equipment from another company hurt you.

If you were hurt as a 1099 worker, don't assume you're out of luck. Get a legal opinion before you accept "we don't have insurance for you" as the final answer.

## How damages differ from workers' comp (no caps on pain/suffering)

Workers' comp is a no-fault system — you get benefits without proving anyone was at fault, but the benefits are capped and limited. Third-party and non-subscriber claims are fault-based — you have to prove negligence, but the recovery is full.

The difference:

| Damage Category | Workers' Comp | Third-Party / Non-Subscriber Claim |
|---|---|---|
| Medical bills | Paid, but capped by state fee schedule | Paid in full, including future care |
| Lost wages | ~70% of average weekly wage, capped | Full lost wages, past and future |
| Lost earning capacity | Limited impairment benefit | Full economic loss, often expert-supported |
| Pain and suffering | **Not available** | Full recovery (no cap in most Texas cases) |
| Mental anguish | **Not available** | Full recovery |
| Disfigurement | Limited | Full recovery |
| Loss of consortium (spouse's claim) | **Not available** | Available |
| Exemplary (punitive) damages | Only in gross-negligence death cases | Available for gross negligence |

In most Texas personal injury cases, there is **no cap** on pain and suffering. (Medical malpractice has a $250,000 cap, but ordinary negligence cases do not. Goff Law does not handle medical malpractice — but most workplace injury cases are ordinary negligence, not medmal.)

This is why third-party recoveries are often several times larger than what workers' comp would pay for the same injury.

## The 2-year clock and the 30-day employer-notice trap

Two deadlines can quietly destroy your case. Both can hit you in the first month after an injury.

**The 30-day employer-notice deadline (Texas Labor Code § 409.001)**

If your employer DOES have workers' comp and you want to preserve any comp benefits, you must report the injury to your employer **within 30 days** of the injury (or within 30 days of when you knew or should have known the injury was work-related). Miss this, and you may lose comp benefits entirely.

Even if you're planning to pursue a third-party claim instead of comp, give written notice to your employer within 30 days anyway. It costs nothing and preserves every option.

**The 2-year statute of limitations for the negligence portion**

Under **Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.003**, you have **two years from the date of injury** to file a third-party negligence lawsuit or a non-subscriber lawsuit. Two years sounds like a long time. It is not — especially when your case requires:

- Identifying every potentially liable party (employers, manufacturers, contractors, drivers, owners)
- Preserving evidence at a job site that may be cleaned up within days
- Securing the defective equipment before it's repaired, scrapped, or modified
- Obtaining surveillance footage before it's overwritten (often 7–30 days)
- Locating witnesses while their memories are fresh and their contact info is current

Cases brought in month 22 are dramatically weaker than the same case brought in month 2. Evidence disappears. Witnesses move. Companies destroy records on routine retention schedules.

If you were hurt at work in Texas and you think a third party may be involved — or your employer may be a non-subscriber — talk to an attorney **this week**, not in two years.
