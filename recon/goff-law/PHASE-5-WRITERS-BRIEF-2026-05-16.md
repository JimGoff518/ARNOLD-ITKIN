## SECTION 1 — Why this phase is flag-planting (read this before you start writing)

These 4 pages are NOT volume plays. They are **URL real-estate plays**. Search volume per page is small right now — roughly 25 to 55 monthly searches each — and the keyword difficulty scores are low (22 to 25) precisely because almost no DFW PI firm has built dedicated pages for these emerging tort categories yet.

**The thesis in one paragraph:** E-scooter, gig-economy, and autonomous-vehicle crashes are growing at 30 to 50% YoY in DFW. The big DFW PI shops — Mullen, Witherite, Reyes, McCraw, Snellings, Aldous — are all still positioned around traditional MVA and trucking. None of them have purpose-built landing pages for these emerging categories. Jim plants the flag now, and when search volume catches up to the underlying crash volume in 18 to 36 months, the URL already ranks because it was first, lived longest, accumulated topical authority, and built internal links from the rest of the Goff Law site. **Cost of waiting = the URL is no longer ours to claim.**

**What this means for how you write:**

- **Do not chase volume.** Write the page as if 100,000 people will eventually read it, even though 30 will read it this month. The page has to be authoritative on Day 1 because it has to survive 36 months of competitor catch-up.
- **Authority over breadth.** Each page is narrow in scope but deep in expertise — especially on the insurance-coverage section, which is the trust anchor (see Section 2).
- **Internal linking is half the value.** These pages will be linked from Phase 1 FAQ pages, Phase 3 mass-tort pages, Phase 4 location pages, and (most importantly) each other. The link equity flowing into and out of these 4 pages is what builds the topical cluster.
- **Don't over-pivot for short-term traffic.** Skip the listicle treatment. Don't write a "10 Things to Know" version. Write the definitive Texas-attorney explainer that a paralegal at another firm would forward to a partner.

5. **City of Dallas / TxDOT** — if a pothole, broken pavement, missing signage, or improperly designed crossing caused the crash. **The Texas Tort Claims Act notice deadline is much shorter than the two-year SOL — often 45 to 90 days under the Dallas city charter.** Miss the notice deadline and the claim against the city is barred regardless of the two-year clock.

**THE TRUST-ANCHOR SECTION — DoorDash three-tier insurance structure (MUST be the longest section on the page):**

**Tier 1 — Offline (not logged into the Dasher app):**
- DoorDash provides ZERO coverage.
- Only available insurance is the driver's personal auto policy.
- Texas minimum limits: **$30,000 per person / $60,000 per accident / $25,000 property damage**.
- Often not enough to cover a single ER visit.

**Tier 2 — Online but no order accepted (driver waiting for a ping):**
- DoorDash carries **limited contingent liability coverage** that kicks in ONLY if the driver's personal auto policy denies the claim first.
- Narrower than the active-delivery policy.
- Has its own deductibles and conditions.

**Tier 3 — Active delivery (order accepted, en route to pickup or drop-off):**
- DoorDash's **$1 million occurrence third-party liability policy** is active. **[⚠ VERIFY CURRENT FIGURE BEFORE PUBLISH — gig-platform insurance limits do get adjusted; the $1M figure has been the standard for a long time but confirm from DoorDash's current published terms or a recent industry-tracker source. If the figure has changed, update the page and the FAQ schema accordingly.]**
- Covers bodily injury to third parties (the people the Dasher hit).
- Plus contingent collision coverage for the Dasher's own vehicle (with a deductible).

**The fight in every DoorDash case is proving which tier was active at the exact moment of impact.** A Dasher who just dropped off and is heading home = Tier 2, not Tier 3. A Dasher who is "waiting for an order" but had not tapped Accept = Tier 2, not Tier 3. **The difference between Tier 2 and Tier 3 is the difference between $30,000 and $1,000,000 in available coverage.**

**The deeper problem — personal-auto commercial-use exclusion:**

Almost every standard Texas personal auto policy contains the standard ISO commercial-use exclusion — language like "use of the vehicle to carry persons or property for compensation or a fee" or "while engaged in any business or occupation involving the transportation of persons or property." **Translation: when the Dasher is delivering, their own personal policy may legally deny the claim.** That's what happens in practice. Personal insurer denies → Dasher is personally on the hook → injured party (the reader) is left chasing whichever tier of DoorDash coverage was active.

**Liability theories:**

**Track 1 — Driver negligence under Level 2:**
- Tesla's user agreement and NHTSA classification put responsibility on the driver.
- Driver's auto policy is the first stop. Same as any car wreck.

**Track 2 — Products liability against Tesla:**
- **Design defect** — Autopilot/FSD as designed is not reasonably safe (stationary-object detection failure, phantom braking, unprotected-left-turn errors).
- **Manufacturing defect** — a specific component (camera, radar, computer) failed.
- **Failure to warn** — Tesla's branding ("Autopilot," "Full Self-Driving") oversold the system's capabilities; drivers reasonably believed the car could handle situations it cannot.
- **Software-update defect** — Tesla pushes over-the-air updates that change driving behavior; an update that introduces a new failure mode is its own products claim.

**Track 3 — Why the products track matters:**
- Normal Texas auto policy caps at $250K–$1M.
- Products-liability claim against a Fortune 500 manufacturer is not subject to that cap.
- **The products angle is what turns a five-figure auto case into a seven-figure products case.**

**Texas tort-cap point (do include this):**
