# Tesla Autopilot Accident Lawyer in Texas

By [Jim Goff](/attorneys/jim-goff/) | Updated 2026-05-16

If you were hurt in a crash where a Tesla was running Autopilot or Full Self-Driving, you are at the leading edge of a new area of personal injury law — one where the question of who is responsible is genuinely contested between the driver, the manufacturer, and the software. As a **Tesla Autopilot accident lawyer in Texas**, I handle these cases across the state — Dallas, Houston, Austin, San Antonio, and the affluent suburbs (Plano, Frisco, Southlake) where Tesla ownership is concentrated. This page explains how liability gets sorted out, what evidence is critical, and why the products-liability angle is often the difference between a five-figure and a seven-figure case.

## How Tesla Autopilot and FSD Crashes Happen in Texas

Tesla's driver-assistance technology comes in several layers — basic Autopilot, Enhanced Autopilot, and Full Self-Driving (FSD) — and the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) classifies all of them as **Level 2 driver-assistance systems**, not autonomous vehicles. Level 2 means the driver is supposed to remain fully attentive and ready to take over at any moment.

That gap between what the technology can actually do and what drivers think it can do is where the crashes happen.

The pattern of Tesla Autopilot crashes documented in NHTSA's open investigations and Standing General Order data — and replicated in Texas — includes:

- **Stationary-object collisions** — Autopilot has had documented difficulty detecting stationary emergency vehicles, construction barriers, parked cars, and crash-scene debris. Texas highway crashes involving Teslas striking parked TxDOT vehicles or stopped traffic at the back of a backup are a recurring fact pattern.
- **Lane-departure events** — sudden steering inputs from the system into adjacent lanes or off the roadway.
- **Phantom braking** — unprompted hard braking at highway speeds, causing rear-end collisions where the Tesla driver is technically the front car but the system caused the stop.
- **Failure to recognize crossing traffic** — at intersections without traffic signals, or where vision-based perception is degraded by sun glare, rain, or construction.
- **FSD city-street errors** — running stop signs, attempting unprotected left turns into oncoming traffic, navigating through cones or work zones improperly.
- **Driver inattention while system is engaged** — drivers misled by branding into thinking the car was "driving itself" and looking away from the road.

Tesla ownership in Texas is concentrated in the affluent suburbs — Plano, Frisco, Allen, Southlake, Westlake, the Woodlands, West University, parts of Austin, and Lake Travis area. That makes Texas highways (I-35, I-45, I-10, US-75, the Dallas North Tollway) high-Tesla-density corridors during commute hours.

## The Insurance Coverage Problem (Why These Cases Are Different)

A Tesla Autopilot crash is genuinely a **dual-track liability case**. There is the ordinary motor-vehicle insurance track and there is a separate products-liability track against Tesla as the manufacturer. Generic personal injury firms in Texas typically only pursue the first one.

**Track 1 — Driver negligence and motor-vehicle liability.**

Tesla's own marketing and user agreement put the legal responsibility for the vehicle's operation on the driver. Autopilot and FSD are Level 2 systems, and the driver is supposed to remain in control. So in the simplest version of the case, the Tesla driver is at fault and the case proceeds against the driver's auto insurance — same as any other car wreck.

**Track 2 — Products liability against Tesla.**

This is where Tesla cases get interesting. Even when the driver had some responsibility, there is often a credible claim against Tesla itself under one or more of these theories:

- **Design defect** — the Autopilot or FSD system as designed is not reasonably safe. Failure to detect stationary objects, phantom braking, and inability to handle certain intersection types are repeating failure modes.
- **Manufacturing defect** — a specific component (camera, radar, computer) failed in the way of an isolated defect.
- **Failure to warn** — Tesla's branding ("Autopilot," "Full Self-Driving") and marketing arguably oversold the system's capabilities and underwarned drivers about the actual limits. Drivers reasonably believed the car could handle situations it cannot.
- **Software-update defect** — Tesla pushes over-the-air software updates that change the car's driving behavior. An update that introduces a new failure mode can be its own products-liability claim.

NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation has multiple open investigations into Tesla's Autopilot system, and Tesla has issued software-update recalls in response. Those NHTSA investigations and recall actions create a documented record that supports the design-defect theory. We monitor NHTSA's actions on Tesla closely because every new finding can strengthen pending and future cases.

**Track 3 — Insurance limits and the dual-track multiplier.**

A normal Texas auto policy might cap out at $250K to $1M. A products-liability claim against Tesla as a Fortune 500 manufacturer is not subject to that cap. **The products-liability track is what turns a Tesla case from a five-figure car wreck into something larger.** For an injured passenger inside a Tesla that crashed itself, or a third-party motorist hit by a Tesla running Autopilot, the products angle is often the entire case.

**The Texas tort-cap point.** Goff Law's lane is federal multidistrict litigation and motor-vehicle products liability — areas where the Texas non-economic damages caps do not apply the way they do to medical malpractice or generic premises liability. Federal products-liability claims against Tesla fall squarely inside that lane.

## Who's Liable in a Texas Tesla Crash?

- **The Tesla driver** — under the user agreement and NHTSA Level 2 classification, the driver is supposed to remain in control. Driver negligence is almost always at least partially in play.
- **Tesla, Inc.** — design defect, manufacturing defect, failure to warn, software-update defect, marketing that misrepresented system capabilities.
- **Another driver** — if a third vehicle contributed to causing the crash.
- **A component supplier** — cameras, radar, batteries, and software libraries inside a Tesla come from third-party suppliers. Direct claims against a component manufacturer are possible in some cases.
- **A property owner or government entity** — for unsafe road conditions, missing signage, or construction-zone hazards that the Autopilot system could not handle.

Texas comparative-fault rules under Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 33.001 apply. You can recover as long as you are less than 51% at fault. In a Tesla case, apportionment fights between "driver" and "manufacturer" are central to the case strategy.

## Evidence That Disappears in Tesla Cases

This is where Tesla cases are unlike any other car wreck — the **vehicle itself is the central piece of evidence, and it can be remotely modified by the manufacturer**.

- **Tesla's onboard event-data recorder (EDR)** — every Tesla logs detailed vehicle state, sensor data, Autopilot/FSD engagement status, driver attention monitoring, steering and brake inputs, and camera triggers. Far more detailed than a typical car's EDR.
- **Tesla's cloud-side logs** — the vehicle uploads telemetry to Tesla's servers. Tesla controls retention and access. Subpoenas and spoliation letters need to go out fast.
- **Software version at time of crash** — the exact firmware version determines what the car was capable of seeing and doing. Over-the-air updates can change this AFTER the crash, potentially affecting the evidence record.
- **Camera footage** — Tesla's "TeslaCam" / Sentry Mode records exterior camera footage. The footage is stored on a USB drive inserted in the vehicle. **Do not let anyone touch the USB drive.** Preserve it as found.
- **Driver attention monitoring records** — Teslas log whether the driver had hands on the wheel and eyes on the road. Tesla often uses this data to argue driver negligence, so the data cuts both ways.
- **Over-the-air update history** — when did Tesla push the last update, what did it change, did the update introduce or remove a known failure mode.
- **NHTSA Standing General Order reports** — Tesla is required to report Autopilot/FSD crashes to NHTSA. Those reports are public records.
- **The Tesla itself** — do not let the vehicle be repaired, scrapped, or sold until our experts have done a forensic inspection. Once the vehicle is gone, the case is gone.
- **Nearby surveillance** — typical Texas highway and intersection cameras overwrite in 7 to 30 days.

The biggest practical risk in a Tesla case: **the car gets towed to a repair shop, the insurance adjuster declares it a total loss, and the vehicle gets sold to salvage** — sometimes overseas — within weeks of the crash. Once the physical vehicle and its onboard logs are gone, the products-liability case is dramatically harder to prove.

## What To Do Right Now If You're Injured

1. **Get medical care within 72 hours**, even if you "feel okay." Adrenaline masks injuries.
2. **Do not let anyone touch the Tesla.** No repairs, no salvage sale, no USB-drive removal, no over-the-air updates accepted on the phone app. Have it towed to a secure storage facility — not the at-fault insurer's preferred shop.
3. **Photograph the touchscreen** — Autopilot/FSD engagement status, software version (Controls → Software), recent alerts.
4. **Save your Tesla app data.** Trip history, software version logs, anything visible in the app.
5. **Get the police to the scene** and ensure the CR-3 notes Autopilot/FSD engagement if you know it was active.
6. **Identify all witnesses.** A bystander who saw the Tesla driving with no driver input is critical.
7. **Do not give a recorded statement** to your insurer, the other driver's insurer, or any Tesla representative.
8. **Do not delete or modify TeslaCam footage** on the USB drive.
9. **Call a lawyer who handles products-liability cases — not just car wrecks.** The spoliation letter to Tesla needs to go out fast.

## Texas Statute of Limitations

Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.003 gives you **two years from the date of the crash** for a personal injury claim. The two-year clock applies to both the negligence claim against the driver and the products-liability claim against Tesla.

There is also a separate **statute of repose for products liability** under Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.012 — generally 15 years from the date the product was sold. For Teslas, this is rarely an issue because the cars are recent. But it matters in cases involving older vehicles or aftermarket components.
