## How Gig Driver Injuries Happen in Dallas

You were driving when someone else's bad decision hurt you. Common scenarios we see:

- **Rear-ended at a delivery pickup** — you are sitting at a Chick-fil-A, McDonald's, or Walmart pickup curb with your hazards on and a distracted driver slams into the back of your car.
- **Side-impact at an apartment complex** — Dallas has hundreds of apartment communities with narrow internal roads and blind corners; right-of-way violations happen constantly.
- **Struck by another delivery driver** — two Dashers, two Uber Eats drivers, or an Amazon Flex driver and a DoorDash driver meeting at the same intersection, all looking at apps.
- **Hit-and-run** — gig drivers stop in unusual places (loading docks, alleyways, fire lanes) and at-fault drivers sometimes flee.
- **DUI or impaired driver** — Dallas has high rates of late-night impaired driving, especially around Uptown, Deep Ellum, Lower Greenville, and the West End.
- **Commercial truck or rideshare collision** — being hit by a commercial vehicle while you are delivering changes the math entirely because commercial policies are much larger.
- **Cargo loading injuries** — back injuries from lifting Amazon Flex packages or heavy Instacart grocery loads at the warehouse or store.

**The bigger picture.** Across all Dallas County roads, TxDOT recorded over 46,000 crashes in 2024 alone. Commercial motor vehicles were involved in 3,857 of them. Gig drivers spend disproportionate time on the road during peak crash hours — meal-delivery surges (5–9 p.m.), late-night bar closings, and weekend lunch.

## The Insurance Coverage Problem (Why These Cases Are Different)

This is the section that separates a lawyer who handles gig-driver cases from one who handles ordinary car wrecks. **You have three coverage problems most working people never face.**

**Problem 1 — You are a 1099 independent contractor. There is no workers' comp.**

Every major gig platform classifies its drivers as independent contractors, not employees. That means **no Texas workers' compensation coverage** for medical bills or lost wages. (Texas does not require most private employers to carry workers' comp anyway, so this is a double hit.) The platforms offer limited "occupational accident" coverage that is much narrower than workers' comp — usually capped, with a deductible, and not covering loss of earning capacity.

**Problem 2 — Your personal auto insurance may deny the claim.**

Almost every standard Texas personal auto policy contains a commercial-use exclusion. The exact wording varies, but the language usually excludes "use of the vehicle to carry persons or property for compensation or a fee" or "while engaged in any business or occupation involving the transportation of persons or property." Translation: **while you were delivering, your personal policy may legally deny coverage** — for your medical bills, for your lost wages, for the damage to your own car.

This is the part most gig drivers do not learn about until they need the coverage and the claim gets denied.

**Problem 3 — The platform's "coverage" is tier-based and has gaps.**

Each platform handles it differently:

- **DoorDash** carries up to $1 million in third-party liability **when you are actively delivering** (Tier 3 — order accepted, en route to drop-off). The coverage protects people YOU hit, not you. There is limited contingent coverage when you are online without an active order.
- **Uber Eats** uses a similar three-tier model with up to $1 million in third-party liability and limited contingent coverage when online without a trip. Uber also carries UM/UIM coverage in many states during active trips — Texas-specific terms vary and have to be read off the current policy.
- **Instacart** carries an occupational accident policy and limited liability coverage that activates during active "batches." The contingent coverage windows are narrow.
- **Amazon Flex** is its own animal. Amazon provides a commercial auto policy that covers the Flex driver during active "blocks" (the scheduled delivery window). Coverage activates when you accept the block and pick up the packages, and ends when the block ends or you finish the route.

**The key recovery paths for an injured gig driver.** When you are hurt while delivering and someone else was at fault, the money usually comes from:

1. **The at-fault driver's auto liability insurance** — the cleanest path. Same as any car wreck.
2. **Your own UM/UIM coverage** (Uninsured / Underinsured Motorist) — if the at-fault driver had no insurance or not enough. **Read your personal auto declarations page carefully.** Texas requires insurers to OFFER UM/UIM but allows it to be rejected in writing. If you have it, this is often the single most valuable layer for a gig driver.
3. **The platform's commercial liability or UM/UIM coverage** (where it applies during the tier you were in at the moment of impact).
4. **Your health insurance** — for medical bills, often with subrogation rights.
5. **A products-liability or premises-liability claim** — if a defective vehicle part, a loading-dock condition, or a warehouse hazard contributed.

The reason a general PI firm leaves money on the table here is they treat a gig-driver wreck like a normal car wreck. They settle against the at-fault driver's $30,000 minimum policy and never investigate the platform contingent coverage, the UM/UIM layer, or the products-liability angle.

## Who's Liable in a Dallas Gig-Driver Crash?

- **The at-fault driver** — direct negligence (speeding, distracted, drunk, ran a light).
- **The at-fault driver's employer** — if they were on the clock for a commercial business, with vicarious liability and commercial coverage.
- **A trucking company** — if a commercial vehicle hit you. Truck-accident cases have much larger coverage (often $1M to $5M+ per occurrence) and federal regulations (FMCSA) apply.
- **A property owner** — for unsafe conditions at a loading dock, parking lot, or apartment complex entry.
- **A vehicle manufacturer** — for a defective brake, tire, or steering component (products liability).
- **The platform** — for contingent coverage during the tier you were in, and rarely for direct theories tied to platform safety policies.

Texas comparative-fault rules under Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 33.001 apply. You can recover as long as you are less than 51% at fault.

## Evidence That Disappears in Gig-Driver Cases

The retention schedules here are brutal. **Most of the platform evidence is gone in 30 to 90 days.**

- **Platform app status and timestamps** — proves which tier was active and whether platform coverage applies. We send spoliation letters to every relevant platform within days.
- **GPS pings and route data** — exact speed, location, and route reconstruction.
- **Order/batch records** — pickup and drop-off details, customer information, merchant timestamps.
- **The at-fault driver's vehicle EDR** — about 20 seconds of pre-crash data; lost if the car is repaired or scrapped.
- **Nearby surveillance** — restaurant cameras, apartment complex gate cameras, warehouse dock cameras. Most overwrite in 7 to 30 days.
- **911 audio and dispatch logs** — TPIA request, some agencies destroy in 90 days.
- **CR-3 crash report** — TxDOT, usually available 10 business days after the wreck.
- **Witness contact info** — phone numbers go stale within a week.
- **Your own medical records and treatment receipts** — keep every prescription, every co-pay, every mileage log to physical therapy.

## What To Do Right Now If You're Injured

1. **Get medical care within 72 hours.** Even if you "feel okay." Adrenaline hides soft-tissue injuries, concussions, and disc damage. The gap between the crash and your first doctor's visit is the single biggest factor adjusters use to discount your case.
2. **Get the police to the scene.** A CR-3 is the foundation of your claim.
3. **Document the delivery context.** Screenshot your active delivery in the app. Photograph any food bag, package, or hot-bag in your car. Save the order confirmation. This is how we prove the active tier for platform coverage.
4. **Do not give a recorded statement** to the at-fault driver's insurance, the platform's insurance, or any "claims investigator." You are not legally required to talk to them.
5. **Do not sign a broad medical authorization.**
6. **Pull your personal auto declarations page.** Check whether you have UM/UIM and whether you have a rideshare/delivery endorsement. This decides which coverage layers are available.
7. **Call a lawyer who specifically handles gig-driver cases** — the platform spoliation letters need to go out fast, and you need someone who knows the tier structures cold.

If you were also injured while doing W-2 work and the employer-vs-third-party question is in play, see our [Injured at Work — Third-Party Claim guide](/faq/injured-at-work-texas-third-party-claim/). Many gig drivers also work W-2 jobs, and the two analyses sometimes overlap.

## Texas Statute of Limitations

Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.003 gives you **two years from the date of the crash** to file a personal injury lawsuit. That deadline applies to the at-fault driver, the platform's commercial coverage, and any products-liability claim.

But the practical deadline is much sooner. Platform GPS, app status, and order records are on a retention schedule that may purge or anonymize records in **60 to 90 days for some data fields**. Surveillance footage usually overwrites in **7 to 30 days**. The spoliation demand has to go out in the first weeks, not the last month.

For more on Texas filing deadlines and exceptions, see our [Statute of Limitations guide](/faq/personal-injury-statute-of-limitations-texas/).
