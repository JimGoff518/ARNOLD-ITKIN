# Overloaded Truck Accident Lawyer in Dallas

By [Jim Goff](/attorneys/jim-goff/) | Updated 2026-05-16

Dallas County recorded 27 fatal commercial-truck crashes in 2024 and 3,857 total CMV-involved crashes — the highest CMV crash count of any Texas county.<sup>[1](#src1)</sup> A growing share of those wrecks trace back to one root cause that is easy to prove if you act fast: the truck was overloaded. If an overweight 18-wheeler caused your crash on I-635, I-20, or any other Dallas freight corridor, an **overloaded truck accident lawyer** has to look past the driver and find the shipper, the loader, and the dispatcher who signed off on the weight. They are usually the deepest pockets in the case.

## Federal and Texas weight limits for commercial trucks

Federal law sets the basic weight ceiling for a commercial truck on the Interstate system at 80,000 pounds gross vehicle weight (GVW) under 23 U.S.C. § 127. The 80,000-pound limit includes the tractor, trailer, fuel, driver, and cargo combined. The law also caps axle loadings: 20,000 pounds on a single axle and 34,000 pounds on a tandem axle.

Texas mirrors and supplements those limits in the Texas Transportation Code. A truck exceeding the federal or state weight maximums must obtain an oversize/overweight permit through the Texas Department of Motor Vehicles before operating on Texas roads. The permit identifies the approved route, sets escort requirements, and limits the hours of operation. A truck running overweight without a permit — or off the permitted route — is operating in violation of state law.

The reason these limits exist is engineering, not paperwork. Highway pavement, bridge spans, and the truck's own brake and tire system are designed around those weight ceilings. When a load exceeds the ceiling, the failure modes are predictable.

## How overloading causes catastrophic crashes

A truck operating above its design weight does not just "feel heavy." Four specific failure mechanisms turn an overweight load into a crash.

**Brake failure.** Every commercial truck brake system is rated for a specific gross vehicle weight. When the load exceeds that rating, the brakes work harder for every stop. Heat builds up faster, brake fade sets in sooner, and on a long downgrade the brakes can fail completely. The classic outcome is a runaway truck at a freeway off-ramp or a hill descent.

**Tire blowouts.** Tires are rated for a specific load per axle. Exceed the rating and the tire runs hotter than designed, the sidewall flexes more than designed, and the tread can separate. A blowout at highway speed throws hundreds of pounds of rubber and steel debris across multiple lanes, and the truck itself often swerves into adjacent traffic.

**Rollover.** An overloaded trailer raises the center of gravity. On a curved exit ramp or a sudden lane change at speed, the trailer can roll. Once a trailer rolls, it sweeps lanes the same way a jackknife does — only often with greater debris field and fuel-spill risk.

**Extended stopping distance.** A truck loaded to its design weight already needs roughly 40% more distance to stop than a passenger car at the same speed. An overloaded truck needs more — sometimes much more. A driver who tries to stop for slowing traffic on I-20 with an overweight load may simply not have the distance.

Any one of these can produce a multi-vehicle wreck. Often more than one happens at the same time: a brake fade triggers a hard pull on the steering, which triggers a rollover, which produces a debris field. Pulling the threads back to the overload is the work.

## Dallas freight corridors and overload risk

Dallas County sits at the intersection of two of the busiest freight corridors in the Southwest. Across all Dallas County interstates — I-20, I-30, I-35E, I-45, I-345, and I-635 — TxDOT recorded 81 fatal crashes and 90 deaths in 2024.<sup>[2](#src2)</sup>

**I-635 (the LBJ Freeway)** is the northern loop through Dallas County and a primary freight route connecting east-west and north-south Interstate flow. Distribution centers in Mesquite, Garland, and northwest Dallas all funnel onto I-635 — and the freeway's mix of managed and un-managed lanes plus heavy commuter volume creates the kind of stop-and-go conditions where an overloaded truck's brake fade becomes deadly.

**I-20 east-west** runs across south Dallas and is the primary corridor for freight coming in from Louisiana and heading west to El Paso. The grade changes and high speed limits make it a typical setting for tire blowouts on overweight rigs.

Both corridors share something in common: the trucks running them are often loaded somewhere in the Metroplex's warehouse-heavy zip codes — Mesquite, Wilmer-Hutchins, Coppell, the Inland Port near I-45. That means the shipper or loader whose decision created the overload is often within Dallas County's own jurisdiction.

## Who is liable when a truck is overloaded — it is rarely just the driver

A truck driver does not weigh his own cargo. He is handed a sealed trailer with a manifest, and unless the load is obviously off-balance, he generally has no way to know it exceeds the weight ceiling. The decisions that cause overloading happen before the driver pulls out of the warehouse.

Five parties to look at:

**The shipper.** The company that loaded the cargo and signed the bill of lading. Federal law under 49 U.S.C. § 14704 and state common-law principles allow a claim against a shipper who knowingly loaded cargo above the truck's rated capacity.

**The loader.** Often a separate logistics company contracted by the shipper. The loader is responsible for distributing weight across the axles and not exceeding the manifest. Improper loading is a recognized basis for direct liability.

**The freight broker.** A broker that arranged the load knowing it exceeded legal weight — or that selected a carrier whose equipment was not rated to carry it — can face direct liability under negligent-selection theories that are increasingly recognized in federal court.

**The motor carrier.** Even if the carrier did not load the trailer, the carrier is responsible under 49 CFR § 392.2 for ensuring its drivers operate vehicles in compliance with state laws — including weight laws. A carrier that systematically pushes drivers to run overweight loads is on the hook for negligent supervision.

**The driver.** A driver who knew the load was over and ran it anyway. Driver liability is the floor, not the ceiling.

The cargo manifest, the bill of lading, and the weight scale tickets are the documentary proof of who knew what and when. Those records are the first preservation target on every overload case.

## The cargo manifest and weight scale tickets — your case in three documents

In an overloaded truck case, three documents do most of the work.

**The bill of lading** identifies the shipper, the consignee, the cargo, the weight as declared by the shipper, and the route. A bill that declares a weight under 80,000 pounds but is contradicted by the actual scale weight is direct evidence of falsification.

**The weight scale ticket** comes from a certified scale (truck stop, port-of-entry, or DOT weigh station) and gives the actual measured weight of the loaded rig. Texas operates fixed and portable weigh stations on every major freight corridor. If the truck was weighed at any point before the crash, the ticket is the smoking gun.

**The dispatch route plan** shows what the carrier scheduled, including any permitted oversize/overweight routing. A truck running an overweight load off the permitted route is operating illegally under Texas Transportation Code § 623.

These records get destroyed quickly. Bills of lading are often kept only as long as the freight insurance file remains open. Scale tickets at private weigh stations may be discarded within weeks. A preservation letter sent within days of the crash, naming the shipper, the broker, and the carrier and identifying each document by category, is what locks the evidence down.

## Why an overload case is a high-value case

Texas does not cap damages on motor-vehicle injury claims. Recovery in a serious overloaded-truck case includes past and future medical bills, lost wages and diminished earning capacity, physical and mental pain, disfigurement, and loss of consortium for a spouse. In wrongful-death claims, surviving family members can recover under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 71.004.

The overload framework also opens punitive damages where the shipper or carrier knew the truck was overweight and dispatched it anyway. Punitive exposure changes settlement dynamics — corporate defendants who would otherwise grind a case out for years move toward the table once they see a clean record showing they knew the load was over and shipped it anyway.
