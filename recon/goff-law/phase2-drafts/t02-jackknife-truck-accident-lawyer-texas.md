# Jackknife Truck Accident Lawyer in Texas

By [Jim Goff](/attorneys/jim-goff/) | Updated 2026-05-16

Texas recorded 546 fatal commercial-truck crashes in 2024, killing 608 people statewide — and Dallas plus Tarrant counties alone produced 46 of them.<sup>[1](#src1)</sup> A meaningful share of those catastrophic wrecks are jackknife crashes: the moment when a tractor and trailer fold against each other and the trailer sweeps across multiple lanes of traffic. If you were hit by a jackknifing 18-wheeler in Texas, a **jackknife truck accident lawyer** has to do more than prove the driver lost control. The real fight is identifying every party whose decision — driver, carrier, cargo loader, or brake manufacturer — caused the trailer to swing.

## What is a jackknife truck accident?

A jackknife happens when the trailer of a tractor-trailer rig pushes forward faster than the tractor pulling it. The tractor and trailer pivot at the fifth-wheel coupling, and the trailer swings out to the side — forming the shape of a folding pocket knife at roughly a 90-degree angle. Once the angle exceeds about 15 degrees, the driver typically cannot recover the rig.

The physics are straightforward. A loaded tractor-trailer can weigh 80,000 pounds (Texas Transportation Code limits and federal rules under 23 U.S.C. § 127 set this as the standard ceiling). When the trailer wheels lock up but the tractor keeps rolling, or when the tractor brakes harder than the trailer, the trailer overruns its tow point. The momentum carries the trailer across two, three, sometimes all the lanes of a Texas freeway — and any car in the sweep zone is hit broadside by tens of thousands of pounds.

The severity is the reason jackknifes have outsized fatality rates. There is no avoiding a 53-foot trailer sweeping at highway speed. Even multi-car pile-ups are common because the trailer often comes to rest blocking the roadway, and following traffic crashes into it seconds later.

## What causes jackknifes — the four primary failures

Jackknife crashes do not just happen. They trace to one of four root causes, and an investigation has to test for all four.

**1. Brake lock-up.** The most common cause. When the trailer's brakes lock before the tractor's, the trailer skids and pushes the tractor sideways. Modern trucks are required to have Anti-Lock Braking Systems (ABS) on the trailer under 49 CFR § 393.55, but ABS only works if it is functional. A trailer ABS warning lamp that the driver ignored, or a brake balance that has drifted out of spec, is a classic jackknife predicate.

**2. Excess speed for conditions.** A driver running 70 mph on dry pavement is one situation. The same driver at 70 mph on a wet I-635 surface, or coming downhill into a corner near downtown Dallas, is courting a jackknife. Texas Transportation Code § 545.351 requires drivers to operate at a speed "reasonable and prudent" for conditions — a separate violation even when posted limits are not exceeded.

**3. Road conditions: rain, ice, oil-slick.** DFW summer thunderstorms produce sudden surface conditions that a fatigued or inattentive driver does not adapt to. Hard braking on a wet first-rain surface (which lifts months of oil onto the roadway) is a textbook jackknife setup.

**4. Cargo imbalance or shift.** When a trailer is loaded with cargo that shifts forward or to one side under braking, the trailer's center of gravity moves in ways that compound any small lock-up. Improper load distribution under 49 CFR § 393.100 (cargo securement) is the route into a third-party claim against the shipper or loader.

## Why liability in a jackknife case is rarely just "the driver"

A jackknife case is almost never a single-defendant case. Five potential parties sit at the table.

**The driver.** For speed, fatigue, inattention, or failing to respond to ABS warning indicators. Driver liability is the floor, not the ceiling.

**The motor carrier (the trucking company).** For negligent training, negligent supervision, negligent maintenance of the trailer brakes, or pushing the driver into an unrealistic schedule that forced the speed and fatigue. The carrier is also vicariously liable for the driver under respondeat superior.

**The cargo loader or shipper.** When the trailer was loaded by a separate company at a warehouse, distribution center, or port, and the load was improperly distributed or improperly secured. Under 49 CFR § 393.100, both the driver AND the shipper share securement responsibility — but the shipper's liability often gets missed by less-experienced lawyers.

**The trailer owner.** Many trailers are owned by a leasing company or a different entity from the tractor. If the trailer's brake system was not maintained, the trailer owner can be a separate defendant.

**The brake or component manufacturer.** When a brake chamber, slack adjuster, ABS sensor, or air valve fails because of a design or manufacturing defect, a product-liability claim opens against the manufacturer. These claims can be the most valuable in the case because product defendants often have the deepest pockets.

The job of a jackknife truck accident lawyer is to identify every one of these parties early, send preservation letters to each, and structure the case so liability is apportioned across all responsible parties. Texas's modified comparative fault rule (Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 33.001) means how blame is divided directly drives what each defendant pays.

## Evidence you must lock down in the first 30 days

Jackknife evidence disappears fast. The carrier will have the truck towed to its own yard within 24 hours. Within a week, the trailer can be back in service. Within 30 days, the Event Data Recorder can be overwritten and the ELD record can quietly disappear during a "routine data refresh."

The first 30-day evidence list:
- Event Data Recorder (the truck's "black box") — captures speed, brake application, throttle position in the seconds before the crash
- ELD data — driver hours, route history, log status changes
- Trailer brake records and DVIRs for the prior 12 months
- ABS fault codes from the trailer's diagnostic system
- Bill of lading and load manifest from the shipper
- Photos of the load distribution before unloading
- Driver's drug and alcohol test results (FMCSA requires post-accident testing under 49 CFR § 382.303)
- Dashcam and inward-facing camera footage if equipped
- Roadway surveillance, business camera, and TxDOT traffic camera footage from the surrounding area
- Witness statements (witnesses disappear within 48 hours)

A preservation letter sent to the carrier within the first week, with a specific list of materials to preserve, is what locks this evidence down. Without it, materials get "lost" — and the carrier will not be sanctioned for spoliation if no preservation demand was ever made.

## What a Texas jackknife case is worth

Texas does not cap damages on motor-vehicle injury claims — one of the reasons truck cases are a focus at Goff Law DFW. Recovery in a serious jackknife case typically includes medical bills (past and future), lost wages and diminished earning capacity, physical and mental pain, disfigurement, and loss of consortium for a spouse. In wrongful-death claims, surviving family members can recover under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 71.004.

Punitive damages are available where the carrier showed gross negligence — for example, putting a driver on the road with a documented brake problem the maintenance department flagged and the dispatcher ignored. Those records exist; the work is getting them produced.

Every case is different and no lawyer can promise an outcome. What we can promise is that we will identify every potentially liable party, send the preservation letters that protect your evidence, and build the case in the federal regulatory framework that gives a Texas truck claim its real value.
