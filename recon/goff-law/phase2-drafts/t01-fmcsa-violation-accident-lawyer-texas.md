# FMCSA Violation Truck Accident Lawyer in Texas

By [Jim Goff](/attorneys/jim-goff/) | Updated 2026-05-16

Texas roads saw 546 fatal commercial-truck crashes in 2024, and Dallas and Tarrant counties alone produced 46 of them — about 8.4% of every fatal CMV crash in the state.<sup>[1](#src1)</sup> Behind a huge share of those wrecks is a Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) violation: an over-hours driver, a tampered electronic logging device, a brake the carrier never repaired. If you were hit by an 18-wheeler in Texas, an **FMCSA violation accident lawyer** can turn a federal regulation into the centerpiece of your case — because in Texas, breaking a safety statute that was written to protect you is treated as evidence of negligence.

## What FMCSA regulations are and why they matter to your claim

The FMCSA is the federal agency that regulates interstate trucking. Its rules live in Title 49 of the Code of Federal Regulations (49 CFR), and they cover everything a trucking company and its drivers have to do to operate legally — from how many hours a driver can spend behind the wheel to how often the brakes get inspected.

Texas adopts most of these federal rules at the state level through the Texas Transportation Code § 644, which gives DPS and TxDPS the authority to enforce federal motor-carrier safety rules on every commercial truck operating in the state — including intrastate carriers. That means even a Texas-only fleet running a route from Dallas to Houston is bound by 49 CFR.

For your injury case, the practical impact is this: when a trucking company or driver violates an FMCSA rule and that violation causes a crash, Texas courts allow that violation to be presented to the jury as evidence of negligence — sometimes as negligence per se. You no longer have to argue that the driver was "careless." You can point to a specific federal rule, show the carrier broke it, and connect the break to your injury.

That is a fundamentally different case from a typical fender-bender. It is technical, it is regulatory, and it requires a lawyer who understands the federal framework — not just Texas tort law.

## Hours of Service (HOS) violations: 49 CFR § 395

The single most common FMCSA violation behind catastrophic truck crashes is an Hours of Service breach. The rules at 49 CFR § 395.3 are strict: a commercial driver can drive a maximum of 11 hours after 10 consecutive hours off duty, cannot drive after the 14th consecutive hour on duty, and cannot exceed 60 hours on duty in 7 consecutive days (or 70 hours in 8 days).

These limits exist because federal research has shown fatigue impairs a driver as much as alcohol. When a carrier pressures a driver to "make the load" past the legal limit — or when a driver fudges the log to keep the paycheck — the result is a tired driver operating an 80,000-pound vehicle on Texas highways.

Common HOS violations we see in DFW truck cases:
- Driver exceeded the 11-hour driving limit
- Driver failed to take the required 30-minute break after 8 hours
- Carrier scheduled a route impossible to complete inside the 14-hour window
- Driver logged "off duty" while actually loading or unloading
- 70-hour weekly limit exceeded across consecutive shifts

Each of these is a hard violation of 49 CFR § 395.3. The proof lives in the driver's Electronic Logging Device data — which is why preservation matters so much.

## ELD tampering and falsified logs

Since December 2017, FMCSA has required most commercial trucks to use an Electronic Logging Device (ELD) under 49 CFR § 395.20. The ELD automatically records driving time by pulling data from the truck's engine. It was supposed to end the era of paper-log fraud.

It didn't. Drivers and carriers tamper with ELDs in several ways: logging into the "personal conveyance" or "yard move" status to mask driving time, using a "ghost driver" co-driver login, or running an outright unregistered device.

When we suspect ELD tampering, the case becomes a forensics matter. We subpoena the ELD vendor's back-end data (not just the carrier's printout), pull engine ECM data from the truck itself, and cross-check both against fuel-stop receipts, toll records, and GPS pings from the cab. Discrepancies between those sources are how falsification gets proven.

## Inadequate driver training, qualification, and supervision

49 CFR Part 391 sets out who a trucking company is legally allowed to put behind the wheel. The carrier must verify the driver has a valid CDL, pull a 3-year Motor Vehicle Record, conduct pre-employment drug and alcohol screening, and check the driver's prior crash and inspection history through FMCSA's Pre-Employment Screening Program (PSP).

Carriers cut corners here constantly — especially when driver shortages push them to hire fast. Common Part 391 failures:
- Hiring a driver with a suspended CDL
- Skipping the pre-employment drug screen
- Ignoring a recent DUI or pattern of moving violations
- Failing to confirm the driver has the proper endorsements (HazMat, doubles/triples, tanker)
- No record of entry-level driver training under 49 CFR Part 380

When a carrier hires an unqualified driver and that driver causes a crash, the claim becomes "negligent hiring" — and the carrier is on the hook directly, not just through respondeat superior.

## Fleet maintenance failures: 49 CFR § 396

A trucking company has to keep its fleet in safe operating condition. 49 CFR § 396 requires systematic inspection, repair, and maintenance of every commercial vehicle, along with detailed records. Drivers must complete a Driver Vehicle Inspection Report (DVIR) every day, and any reported defect that affects safety must be repaired before the truck rolls again.

When you see a tractor-trailer rollover with brake-line failure, or a tire blowout that triggers a multi-vehicle pile-up on I-635, the question is almost always: when was the last inspection, what did the DVIR say, and did the carrier actually fix what was reported? The maintenance file — required to be kept for 12 months per § 396.3(c) — tells the story. If the file is missing or the records are sparse, that itself is evidence of negligence.

## FMCSA SMS scores and the BASIC categories

FMCSA publishes a public Safety Measurement System (SMS) for every motor carrier. The system scores carriers across seven Behavior Analysis and Safety Improvement Categories (BASICs): Unsafe Driving, Hours-of-Service Compliance, Driver Fitness, Controlled Substances/Alcohol, Vehicle Maintenance, HazMat Compliance, and Crash Indicator.

When we open a new truck-accident case at Goff Law DFW, the first step is pulling the carrier's SMS profile. A carrier flagged with above-threshold scores in Unsafe Driving or Vehicle Maintenance enters the case with a regulatory paper trail that already documents a pattern. That pattern is admissible to show the carrier knew (or should have known) it had a safety problem and did nothing. It transforms a single-crash case into a corporate-conduct case — and corporate-conduct cases get the kind of punitive-damages exposure that motivates real settlements.
