# Truck Driver Fatigue Accident Lawyer in Texas

By [Jim Goff](/attorneys/jim-goff/) | Updated 2026-05-16

Texas saw 546 fatal commercial-truck crashes in 2024, with 608 people killed statewide — Dallas and Tarrant counties combined for 46 of those fatal CMV wrecks, or 8.4% of the state total in two counties alone.<sup>[1](#src1)</sup> Federal safety researchers have identified driver fatigue as a factor in roughly 13% of large-truck crashes — and likely more once underreporting is accounted for. If you were hit by a drowsy 18-wheeler driver in Texas, a **truck driver fatigue accident lawyer** has to do one thing fast: subpoena the right records before the trucking company "loses" them.

## What truck driver fatigue actually means in a Texas case

Truck driver fatigue is not just "the driver was tired." It is a regulated condition with a federal definition. The FMCSA recognizes fatigue as impairment severe enough to require federal rules limiting how long a commercial driver can be on duty in a single shift and across a workweek.

The reason: federal research has shown fatigue impairs reaction time, judgment, and lane-keeping at levels comparable to alcohol intoxication. A driver who has been awake for 18 hours performs roughly like a driver at 0.05% BAC. After 24 hours, the impairment approaches 0.10% BAC — over the Texas legal limit for non-commercial drivers, and well past the 0.04% limit that applies to commercial drivers under 49 CFR § 392.5.

When a tractor-trailer driver causes a Texas crash because they fell asleep, drifted across lanes, or failed to react to slowing traffic, fatigue is almost always the underlying explanation. The investigation question is: did the carrier or driver violate federal Hours of Service rules to make that fatigue inevitable?

## Hours of Service rules under 49 CFR § 395.3

The federal Hours of Service rules at 49 CFR § 395.3 set hard limits on driving time for property-carrying commercial drivers:

- **11-hour driving limit.** A driver can drive a maximum of 11 hours after 10 consecutive hours off duty.
- **14-hour duty limit.** A driver cannot drive after the 14th consecutive hour on duty. The clock keeps running even during loading, unloading, fueling, and breaks.
- **30-minute break rule.** A driver must take a 30-minute break before driving more than 8 cumulative hours.
- **60/70-hour weekly limit.** A driver cannot exceed 60 hours on duty in 7 consecutive days, or 70 hours in 8 consecutive days, depending on whether the carrier operates daily.
- **34-hour restart.** After hitting the weekly limit, the driver must take 34 consecutive hours off duty to reset.

These are not suggestions. They are federal regulations adopted into Texas law through Texas Transportation Code § 644, which authorizes DPS to enforce federal motor-carrier safety rules against every commercial truck operating in the state — including intrastate Texas-only carriers.

When a driver violates one of these limits and a crash follows, the violation is admissible as evidence of negligence and in many Texas cases is treated as negligence per se.

## Electronic Logging Devices and why the 6-month FMCSA window matters

Since December 2017, most commercial trucks have been required to use an Electronic Logging Device (ELD) under 49 CFR § 395.20. The ELD plugs into the truck's engine control module and automatically captures driving time — driver login, duty status, engine hours, miles driven, location at the start and end of each duty period.

Here is the part that matters for your case. Under 49 CFR § 395.22(i), motor carriers are only required to retain ELD records for **6 months**. After that window closes, the carrier has no legal obligation to keep them. In practice, that means anything not preserved in the first six months can — and often does — vanish.

If you wait three months to call a lawyer, you have already burned half the window. If you wait nine months, the data is gone. A truck driver fatigue case that does not include the driver's actual hours log is a case fighting blind.

A lawyer who handles these cases sends a written preservation demand to the carrier within days of being retained, identifying by 49 CFR section every record the carrier is required to keep. The demand creates a legal duty to preserve and exposes the carrier to spoliation sanctions if it "accidentally" loses anything afterward.

## What evidence proves fatigue — beyond the ELD

The ELD is the anchor, but it is rarely the only proof. A complete fatigue case pulls from multiple data sources because trucking companies and drivers have figured out workarounds for the ELD that have to be cross-checked against independent records.

Records we subpoena in a Texas truck driver fatigue case:

- **ELD raw data file** (not the carrier's printout) directly from the ELD vendor's back-end servers, including all edit-history logs
- **Truck Engine Control Module (ECM) data** — speed, engine hours, ignition cycles, fault codes
- **Fuel-stop receipts and credit-card timestamps** — proves where the truck actually was at specific times
- **Toll-tag transponder records** (Texas TxTag, HCTRA, NTTA) showing entry and exit times at specific points
- **GPS pings from the dispatch system** — cab-mounted GPS that runs separately from the ELD
- **Cell-phone records and dispatch text messages** showing communication with the driver during off-duty hours
- **Bills of lading and pickup/delivery confirmations** — establishes the actual schedule the driver had to meet
- **Dashcam and inward-facing camera footage** — many fleets run forward-facing cameras that capture lane drift, hard braking, and following distance
- **Post-accident drug and alcohol test results** under 49 CFR § 382.303 (mandatory within 8 hours for alcohol, 32 hours for drugs after a qualifying crash)
- **Sleep apnea screening records** — if the driver had untreated sleep apnea and the carrier knew or should have known, the case adds a negligent retention claim

Cross-referencing these sources is what catches a falsified log. If the ELD shows the driver "off duty" at a truck stop in Wichita Falls but the fuel-card receipt puts the truck at a stop in Abilene at the same time, the log is fiction.

## When the carrier is liable, not just the driver

A fatigue crash is rarely a "rogue driver" case. The driver did not invent the schedule. Somebody at the carrier built a route plan that required driving beyond the federal limit, or dispatched a driver who had not had the required off-duty time, or pressured the driver to skip the 30-minute break to make a delivery window.

That makes the carrier directly liable, not just vicariously liable through the driver. Direct-liability theories in Texas fatigue cases include:

- **Negligent dispatching** — the dispatcher knew or should have known the schedule could not be met legally
- **Negligent supervision** — the carrier had records showing the driver was already at or near HOS limits and let the trip happen anyway
- **Negligent hiring or retention** — the carrier hired a driver with a known fatigue or sleep-disorder history, or kept a driver who had prior HOS violations
- **Negligent training** — the driver was never properly trained on log-completion under 49 CFR § 395.11

Direct-liability claims matter because they open the door to punitive damages where the carrier showed conscious disregard for safety. Compensatory damages cover the bill. Punitives are what motivate a corporate defendant.

## Texas fatigue-related driving rules

Beyond federal HOS, Texas has its own statute that comes into play. Texas Transportation Code § 545.412 (commonly applied to impaired-by-fatigue driving along with alcohol/drugs) and the broader prohibition on driving while incapable of safely operating a vehicle work alongside the federal rules. A driver who fell asleep behind the wheel in Texas may be subject to additional citation or criminal exposure depending on the facts — which strengthens the civil case.

Each piece compounds the other. The federal violation is one anchor. The state violation is another. Together they build a case that is hard for the carrier's insurer to defend against without writing a settlement check.
