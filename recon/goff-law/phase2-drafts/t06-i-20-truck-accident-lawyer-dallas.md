# I-20 Truck Accident Lawyer in Dallas

By [Jim Goff](/attorneys/jim-goff/) | Updated 2026-05-16

Across both Dallas County and Tarrant County interstates combined, TxDOT recorded **131 fatal crashes and 149 deaths in 2024 alone** — roughly one Interstate fatal crash every 67 hours across the two counties. I-20 runs straight through both, connecting east Dallas County (Mesquite, Hutchins) to south Tarrant County (Arlington, south Fort Worth) and tying into the long-haul corridors that link Atlanta to Los Angeles. If an 18-wheeler hit you on I-20, this page explains why this corridor matters, who can be held responsible, and what evidence has to be preserved immediately.

## Why I-20 is Especially Dangerous for Trucks

I-20 is a primary east-west freight artery across the southern United States. Its DFW segment is where transcontinental truck traffic from I-20 meets the north-south flow on I-35E, I-45, the loop traffic on I-635 and I-820, and the I-30 connector to Fort Worth. Drivers reaching the I-20 / I-35E interchange in south Dallas or the I-20 / I-820 interchange in south Tarrant are often nearing the end of long hauls — which puts them deeper into federal Hours-of-Service exposure under [49 CFR § 395](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-395) and closer to fatigue-related mistakes.

Geographically, I-20's DFW corridor crosses several risk-multiplying features: the merging traffic at the I-20 / I-35E split in south Dallas, the multi-level stack at the I-20 / I-820 junction, the heavy commercial corridor through Grand Prairie and Arlington, and the long uphill grade approaching the Trinity River bridges. Each of those features changes truck handling — uphill grades reduce braking effectiveness, multi-level interchanges create steep curves that test rollover stability, and short merge zones force lane changes into blind spots.

I-20 is also the main truck route for distribution centers across south Dallas County (Lancaster, Wilmer, Hutchins) and south Tarrant (Arlington). That means the corridor sees heavy concentrations of regional drayage trucks plus long-haul tractor-trailers — two different driving cultures sharing the same lanes at the same speeds. The result is a corridor where high speed differentials, heavy freight, and complex geometry stack the deck against passenger-vehicle occupants when something goes wrong.

## Common Causes of Truck Crashes on I-20

The fact patterns we see on I-20 truck cases tend to fall into a handful of categories:

- **Driver fatigue at the end of a long haul.** I-20 is a transcontinental corridor; drivers approaching DFW from Shreveport or Midland have often been driving for the full 11-hour daily limit allowed under [49 CFR § 395.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-395/subpart-B/section-395.3) before they reach Dallas County.
- **Unsafe lane changes at interchange merges.** The I-20 / I-35E and I-20 / I-820 merges are documented blind-spot crash points. Sideswipes and override crashes happen when truck drivers change lanes without confirming the lane is clear.
- **Brake failure and maintenance shortcuts.** Texas Transportation Code § 644 adopts the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations, which require carriers to inspect and maintain brakes. Carriers that skip inspections to keep equipment rolling shift liability onto themselves.
- **Speeding for conditions.** A loaded tractor-trailer at 70 mph on wet I-20 pavement near the Trinity bridges needs significantly more stopping distance than a passenger car. Speeding "for conditions" is one of the most frequent contributing factors in CMV fatal crashes.
- **Improperly secured cargo.** Drayage trucks from south Dallas distribution centers sometimes leave a yard with cargo securement that does not meet federal load-securement standards. A load shift on a curve can roll a trailer.

## Who's Liable When a Truck Crash Happens on I-20

Truck crashes on I-20 rarely involve only the driver. Because I-20 is a long-haul corridor with distribution-center traffic feeding in from south Dallas and south Tarrant, multiple parties usually share responsibility:

- **The driver** — for negligent driving, HOS violations, distracted driving, or impairment.
- **The motor carrier** — for negligent hiring, inadequate training, failure to maintain equipment, or pressuring drivers to violate HOS rules to hit a delivery deadline.
- **The cargo loader, shipper, or distribution center** — for overloading, improper securement, hazardous-materials violations, or scheduling routes that force drivers to exceed federal hours.
- **The maintenance contractor** — for skipped or negligent brake, tire, or steering inspections.
- **The truck or component manufacturer** — for defective brakes, tires, steering, or stability systems. Defective-component cases sometimes feed into federal MDL proceedings.

Federal regulations require motor carriers to carry minimum liability insurance ranging from $750,000 (general freight) to $5,000,000 (certain hazardous materials). A catastrophic I-20 crash can exceed those minimums fast, which is why identifying every layer of corporate liability is so important. The trucking company is rarely the only deep pocket.

## Evidence That Disappears Within Days of an I-20 Truck Crash

The trucking company's response team is on the road to the crash site within hours. Their job is to control the evidence. Yours — through your lawyer — is to lock it down first. The fastest-disappearing items:

- **Electronic Logging Device (ELD) data.** Federal law under [49 CFR § 395.22](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-395/subpart-B/section-395.22) only requires carriers to retain ELD records for 6 months. "Lost" or "corrupted" ELD records are a recurring theme in truck litigation.
- **Engine Control Module (ECM) / "black box" data.** Modern truck ECMs record speed, throttle, brake application, and steering input in the moments before a crash. If the truck is repaired or scrapped, that data is destroyed.
- **Dashcam footage.** Many fleets operate forward-facing and driver-facing cameras with auto-overwrite cycles as short as 7 days.
- **Driver Qualification File and prior HOS logs.** Federal law requires the carrier to maintain a file documenting medical certifications, road tests, and prior violations. These files often reveal negligent hiring or a pattern of HOS abuse.
- **Post-crash drug and alcohol test results.** FMCSA requires post-crash testing within 8 hours (alcohol) and 32 hours (drugs) for any fatality or recordable crash.
- **TxDOT highway camera footage and surveillance video from distribution centers, truck stops, and gas stations along I-20.** These commonly overwrite on cycles of 72 hours to 30 days.

A formal **spoliation letter — sent within the first 30 days** — puts the carrier on legal notice that destroying this evidence exposes them to sanctions and adverse-inference jury instructions. That letter is one of the highest-value pieces of paper produced in the early life of an I-20 truck case.

## What To Do If You're in a Truck Crash on I-20

1. **Call 911 immediately.** Any wreck involving a commercial truck on I-20 needs a police response and a CR-3 crash report.
2. **Get safely clear of moving traffic.** I-20 lanes move freight at highway speeds; never stand in a lane to take photos.
3. **Photograph everything.** Truck DOT number on the cab door, all trailer placards (especially any hazmat diamonds), license plates, debris field, skid marks, scene wide-shots, and your own injuries.
4. **Get medical care within 72 hours.** Adrenaline masks soft-tissue, head, and disc injuries. A clean medical record from the first 72 hours is one of the biggest factors in claim value.
5. **Do NOT give a recorded statement to the trucking company's insurance.** They will call within 24 hours. Tell them to direct all communication to your attorney.
6. **Call a truck accident lawyer the same day.** ELD data, dashcam footage, and surveillance video on I-20 start disappearing within a week.
