## How Paragard Became the Subject of Federal Litigation

The Paragard copper IUD was first approved by the FDA in 1984 and has been marketed continuously in the United States since then. It is a small, T-shaped polyethylene device wrapped in copper wire, inserted into the uterus by a trained healthcare provider, designed to prevent pregnancy for up to ten years through the spermicidal effect of copper ions. Paragard's marketing has emphasized that it is the only IUD that provides long-term contraception without hormones, making it particularly attractive to women who experienced side effects with hormonal birth control or who wanted a non-hormonal option for medical or personal reasons.

The device was originally manufactured by FEI Products, then sold to Duramed Pharmaceuticals, then acquired by Teva Pharmaceuticals in 2008, and most recently sold to CooperSurgical (a subsidiary of The Cooper Companies) in 2017. Both Teva and CooperSurgical are now primary defendants in the federal litigation, with Teva responsible for the period when most of the lawsuits' devices were manufactured and CooperSurgical responsible for the current product.

### The Breakage Problem

The clinical complaint at the center of MDL 2974 is that Paragard's design — specifically the arms of the T-shape, which are made of plastic wrapped in copper wire — can become brittle during the years the device sits in the uterus. When a healthcare provider attempts a routine removal (which is supposed to be a straightforward in-office procedure taking minutes), the arm can fracture, leaving a fragment embedded in the uterine wall. In some cases, the broken piece migrates through the uterine wall and ends up in the abdominal cavity, bladder, or other pelvic organs.

The injuries that follow include:

- **Uterine perforation** — punctures or tears in the uterine wall that may require surgical repair
- **Embedded fragments** — broken pieces of the copper arm or core that become lodged in the uterus and require surgical retrieval
- **Migration of fragments** — broken pieces that move to other organs in the pelvic cavity
- **Surgical removal procedures** — including hysteroscopy, laparoscopy, laparotomy, and in some cases hysterectomy
- **Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)** — secondary infection following retained fragments
- **Chronic pelvic pain** — long-term pain from scarring or retained fragments
- **Scar tissue formation (adhesions)** — affecting uterus, fallopian tubes, ovaries
- **Loss of fertility** — including primary infertility from scarring or hysterectomy, and secondary infertility from damage to reproductive structures
- **Repeat surgical procedures** — many women require multiple surgeries to fully retrieve all fragments and address resulting complications

### What the Manufacturers Allegedly Knew

The federal litigation's central allegation is that Teva and the predecessor manufacturers received reports of Paragard breakage incidents over many years but did not adequately warn doctors or patients about the breakage risk in the product labeling, the Patient Information sheet, or the Healthcare Professional Insert. Discovery in MDL 2974 has surfaced internal documents and adverse event reports that the litigation alleges show the manufacturers were aware of the breakage pattern but characterized it as user error (improper removal technique) rather than as a design defect inherent to the device.

The legal theories in MDL 2974 include design defect, manufacturing defect, failure to warn, breach of warranty, fraud, and negligent misrepresentation. The litigation is filed against Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, CooperSurgical Inc., and related corporate entities.

## What You Need to Know If You Are Considering a Claim

Filing a Paragard claim does not require you to sue your OB-GYN, the women's health clinic where the device was inserted or removed, the hospital where surgery occurred, or any healthcare provider who treated you. The defendants are the chemical manufacturers — Teva Pharmaceuticals and CooperSurgical — who designed, manufactured, and marketed the device. Your healthcare providers are not on trial.

There is no out-of-pocket cost. Paragard cases are handled on contingency: no fee unless your case recovers compensation, at which point your attorney is paid from the settlement.

You do not need to have saved the broken IUD fragments (though if your provider gave them to you, keep them). Operative reports, imaging studies (ultrasound, X-ray, CT scan showing the device or fragments), pathology reports from any retrieval surgery, and your medical records documenting the breakage event are the primary documentation.

You do not have to travel to Georgia. The MDL is centralized procedurally in the Northern District of Georgia, but individual claimants do not appear in court unless their case is selected for bellwether trial. Most claimants' direct involvement is limited to providing medical records and (in some cases) a single deposition that can be conducted remotely or in Dallas.

Filing does not affect your health insurance, employer benefits, or any government benefits. Settlement amounts may need to satisfy medical liens, but this is normal claim resolution and your attorney handles it.
