## Why North Texas Suburb Firefighters Have Distinct Exposure Profiles

North Texas suburb firefighters present a slightly different AFFF exposure profile than their Dallas or Houston counterparts, for reasons specific to suburban fire service:

**Mutual aid coverage of high-risk facilities.** Suburb fire departments routinely respond as mutual aid to incidents at DFW Airport, Love Field, the regional natural gas pipeline corridors, the chemical and food-processing facilities in Garland and Mesquite, the petroleum storage at North Texas distribution terminals, and the major freeway corridors where fuel-tanker incidents occur. A Plano or Frisco firefighter who never set foot on DFW Airport property may have responded to dozens of AFFF-required fires as mutual aid backup over a career.

**Regional training academy exposure.** Most North Texas suburb firefighters trained at one of several regional fire academies that conducted live-fire AFFF drills as part of certification: Collin College's Public Safety Training Center (McKinney), Tarrant County College's Fire Service Training Center (Hurst), the North Central Texas Council of Governments Regional Fire Academy, and the Texas A&M Engineering Extension Service (TEEX) Fire School in College Station. TEEX in particular has documented decades of AFFF use for both initial certification and continuing education, meaning North Texas firefighters who recertified annually accumulated training-related exposure on top of operational exposure.

**Industrial and aviation responses.** North Texas's industrial corridor along the I-35E and I-635 freeway routes hosts manufacturing, warehousing, and distribution facilities that require petroleum-product fire suppression capability. Corporate aviation facilities at Addison Airport, McKinney National Airport, Alliance Airport, and Dallas Executive Airport all use or have historically used AFFF for ramp and hangar fire response. Suburb fire departments providing first-response coverage to these facilities deployed AFFF more frequently than the broader public assumes.

**Bunker gear cross-contamination.** Modern research has established that PFAS in AFFF cross-contaminates firefighter bunker gear, station carpet, station ventilation systems, and even firefighters' personal vehicles after foam responses. North Texas suburb firefighters who wore the same bunker gear over years of service — without the rigorous decontamination protocols that some larger metropolitan departments later adopted — accumulated PFAS exposure beyond just the moments of direct foam handling.

## Why North Texas Suburb Firefighter Cases Are Time-Sensitive

Three reasons North Texas suburb firefighters should not delay reviewing potential AFFF claims:

**Departmental records degradation.** Smaller suburb fire departments sometimes have less comprehensive historical records than large metropolitan departments. Training rosters, mutual aid logs, and equipment inventories from the 1970s and 1980s can be incomplete or already lost. Every year that passes makes documentation harder to recover for older exposure periods. If you served in the 1980s or earlier, prompt action on documentation gathering matters more than for newer service years.

**Texas statute of limitations.** The two-year statute runs from the date of diagnosis OR the date the firefighter knew or should have known the cancer was linked to AFFF (the discovery rule). The discovery rule is fact-specific and contested by defendants. Filing earlier, while diagnosis-to-knowledge timelines are still demonstrably recent, is safer than relying on extended discovery-rule arguments.

**MDL settlement structures forming now.** As bellwether trials proceed and global settlement frameworks emerge over the next 18-36 months, qualifying criteria and documentation requirements may tighten. Claims filed earlier in the MDL process typically benefit from more flexible documentation standards.
