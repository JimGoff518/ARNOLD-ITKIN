The Houston metropolitan area concentrates more petrochemical infrastructure than any other region in the United States — and the firefighters who protected that infrastructure for the last six decades carry some of the highest cumulative AFFF (Aqueous Film Forming Foam) exposure of any firefighter population in the country. From the Houston Ship Channel refineries to Texas City's industrial cluster, from Bush Intercontinental Airport's ARFF operations to NASA Johnson Space Center's specialized fire service, Houston-area firefighters were trained with and routinely deployed AFFF because the alternative — using water alone on petroleum, jet fuel, and chemical fires — was either ineffective or actively dangerous.

What those firefighters did not know was that the foam they trusted to save their lives in petroleum-product fires contained per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) — chemicals that 3M, DuPont, Chemours, Tyco, and other manufacturers had internally documented as bioaccumulative and carcinogenic by the 1970s, but continued selling for decades without warning firefighters or fire departments. Today, Houston-area firefighters who served from the 1960s through the 2010s are being diagnosed with kidney cancer, testicular cancer, thyroid cancer, liver cancer, and ulcerative colitis at rates the federal AFFF litigation now ties directly to that documented PFAS exposure.

## Why Houston Has the Most Concentrated AFFF Exposure in Texas

Houston's role as the petrochemical capital of the United States gave Houston-area firefighters a fundamentally different exposure profile than firefighters elsewhere in Texas or the nation. Three factors compound to make Houston firefighter AFFF exposure unusually intense:

**First, the petrochemical density.** The Houston Ship Channel and surrounding industrial cluster contains more than 25% of the nation's refining capacity. Pasadena, Deer Park, Texas City, Baytown, La Porte, and the East End industrial corridor host major operations of ExxonMobil, Shell, LyondellBasell, Phillips 66, Marathon, Valero, Chevron Phillips Chemical, and dozens of other petrochemical and specialty chemical manufacturers. Every one of these facilities has an emergency response team or contract fire brigade that trained with and deployed AFFF for petroleum and chemical fire response. When facility fires occurred — and they did, regularly — Houston-area municipal firefighters were called as mutual aid. Each of those events involved significant AFFF deployment by multiple agencies.

**Second, the aviation infrastructure.** George Bush Intercontinental Airport (IAH) is among the busiest airports in the United States. William P. Hobby Airport (HOU) hosts substantial commercial and private aviation. Both airports' ARFF (Aircraft Rescue and Firefighting) crews trained with AFFF as the required primary suppression agent for aviation fuel fires from each airport's opening through the late 2010s. Additionally, Ellington Field — historically NASA's primary airfield and now a Joint Reserve Base — hosted firefighter training operations that involved AFFF for decades.

**Third, NASA Johnson Space Center.** NASA JSC maintained specialized fire and rescue capability for spacecraft and rocket fuel emergencies, which required specialized AFFF formulations. NASA fire service personnel and contractors who trained with and maintained these capabilities for decades accumulated AFFF exposure that is uniquely well-documented because of NASA's general safety-systems record-keeping rigor.

**Fourth, the refinery emergency response model.** Major Houston-area refineries maintain on-site emergency response teams — sometimes called "refinery firefighters" or "industrial fire brigades" — that respond to facility emergencies before municipal mutual aid arrives. These teams trained with AFFF more frequently than most municipal firefighters because their job was specifically petroleum-fire suppression. Pasadena, Deer Park, Texas City, and Baytown emergency response teams in particular have among the highest documented AFFF exposure of any firefighter population in the United States.

## What AFFF Exposure Has Done to Houston-Area Firefighters' Bodies

The federal AFFF MDL formally recognizes six qualifying medical conditions linked to PFAS exposure:

- **Kidney cancer** — the most common qualifying condition
- **Testicular cancer** — particularly significant in younger firefighters with extended ARFF or refinery training
- **Thyroid cancer** — papillary thyroid cancer most frequently linked
- **Liver cancer** — including hepatocellular carcinoma
- **Thyroid disease and hypothyroidism**
- **Ulcerative colitis**

For Houston-area firefighters, the cumulative exposure documentation matters enormously. A Houston Fire Department firefighter who served thirty years on engine companies handling petroleum fires, plus served in a refinery emergency response team capacity, plus did mutual aid for industrial incidents, accumulates an exposure profile that produces some of the highest PFAS blood serum levels documented in any firefighter population. That intense cumulative exposure typically corresponds to stronger documented claims under the federal MDL's medical nexus requirements.

### The Refinery Firefighter Subpopulation

Refinery emergency response team members deserve a specific note because their exposure pattern differs from municipal firefighters in ways that matter for litigation. Refinery firefighters:

- Trained with AFFF more frequently than municipal firefighters because petroleum fire response was their core mission
- Often worked in environments with ongoing low-level vapor exposure that interacted with PFAS in firefighting gear and equipment
- Sometimes had less rigorous decontamination protocols than larger municipal departments, particularly in earlier decades
- Are often less well-known to the general public as "firefighters" because their work was facility-specific rather than community-facing

If you served as a refinery emergency response team member at any major Houston-area petrochemical facility, you almost certainly qualify under the AFFF MDL exposure criteria if you have a qualifying medical diagnosis. Your facility employer kept records — training logs, equipment maintenance records, incident response reports — that document your exposure history. Your attorney can recover those records through subpoena and discovery as part of building your claim.

## The Federal AFFF Litigation: Current Status (April 2026)

All federal AFFF lawsuits have been consolidated into MDL 2873 before Judge Richard M. Gergel in the District of South Carolina. **15,222 individual claims** were pending as of April 2026.

A bellwether trial scheduled for October 2025 was taken off the calendar so the court could review the surge in recent filings and verify each case meets the medical and exposure proof requirements. New bellwether dates are expected as that review completes.

Legal analysts project settlement values between **$20,000 and $600,000 per qualifying claim**. Houston-area refinery and high-exposure ARFF claims with strong documented exposure history tend toward the higher end of the projected range.

## What You Need to Know If You Are Considering a Claim

Filing an AFFF personal injury claim does not require you to sue Houston Fire Department, your refinery employer, the City of Houston, or any other government or industrial entity. For municipal firefighters, filing does not affect your pension, workers' compensation, or city health insurance.

For NASA and federal civilian firefighters, filing does not affect FERS retirement, federal health benefits, or any other federal employment benefit.

There is no out-of-pocket cost. AFFF claims are handled on contingency: no fee unless your case recovers compensation, at which point your attorney is paid from the settlement.
